\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:2}

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:2.1}

\textbf{Empathy as something that can be measured:} In everyday use empathy is a vague word, and a system cannot be built against a vague word. The framework on which this work is based is EPITOME for text-based mental-health support, which is based on well-known empathy scales, and that divides a reply into three different ways of communicating: \textit{emotional reactions}, naming and validating the person's feelings; \textit{interpretations}, showing that it has understood the situation; \textit{explorations}, inviting the person to say more \cite{sharma2020epitome}. Counselling practice imposes a limit in the opposite sense: it is advised that the 'question and answer trap' occurs when the person becomes a passive respondent to the questions in a series \cite{samhsa2019tip35}. When combined they provide a working definition of a good supportive response that a machine can be held to: acknowledge the feeling, respond to the specific situation, and invite without interrogating.

\textbf{The corpus:} EmpatheticDialogues is the standard benchmark for this task \cite{rashkin2019}. One of 32 emotion labels was given to one crowdworker who wrote a description of a situation and then described the situation in conversation, while the second worker replied as a listener without getting the emotion label. The released files contain 24,850 conversations, segmented into training, validation and test sets, with no speaker being present in more than one of the sets \cite{ed_github}. Two characteristics of the corpus matter in this thesis: the situation description and the first message are two distinct texts with the situation description text generated prior to the first message in the conversation and each conversation has exactly one emotion label which is selected before the conversation is composed.

\textbf{GoEmotions }is the other dataset commonly used for fine-grained emotion, containing about 58,000 Reddit comments labelled with 27 emotions plus neutral, and allowing more than one label per comment \cite{demszky2020}. This project started on GoEmotions and was transferred to EmpatheticDialogues; four reasons for this transfer are measured in Section 4.3.1.

\textbf{Emotion recognition in conversation:} As compared to sentence level emotion classification, emotion recognition in conversation is a field in itself because the same word can have different emotions based on previous words \cite{poria2019erc}. Related work classifies the intents that a listener's reply can express, based on the same corpus \cite{welivita2020} and demonstrates that emotions in dialogue are often implied and thus can only be inferred rather than recognised \cite{koga2023implicit} and often more than one, with one reply carrying more than one label \cite{belay2025}.

\textbf{Models:} All the models in this work are derived from the transformer \cite{vaswani2017}. Two different jobs are performed using two families. \textbf{Encoders }are trained with masked-language modelling, which makes them suitable for classification; BERT set the precedent \cite{devlin2019} and RoBERTa extended it by training on more text, longer and with dynamic masking \cite{liu2019roberta}. \textbf{Decoder-only language models} are models that generate text from left to right and when instruction-tuned, follow written instructions, we use the Qwen2.5 \cite{qwen2024} and Phi-4-mini \cite{abouelenin2025phi4mini} models, with the latter being trained with a focus on following written instructions.

\textbf{Two techniques bring large models to size:} Making a multi-billion-parameter generator usable on a consumer GPU. \textbf{Quantisation }stores weights at reduced precision; 4-bit NF4 with double quantisation retains the quality of 16-bit weights at roughly a quarter of the memory \cite{dettmers2023qlora}. \textbf{Low-rank adaptation} adapts a small set of extra parameters rather than the entire network and is therefore feasible to fine tune on the same hardware \cite{hu2022lora}. A third technique, \textbf{retrieval-augmented generation}, provides a model with relevant passages retrieved at inference time \cite{lewis2020rag} and is typically implemented using sentence embeddings \cite{reimers2019sbert} -- this work uses it as a baseline but does not incorporate it into the final design.

\textbf{Measure of empathy:} No single automatic measure is able to capture empathy, so three types of evaluation have been used here. The\textbf{ WASSA shared task on empathy detection }is a model trained on human empathy ratings of conversation turns \cite{giorgi2024wassa} which is used here as hyy33 system \cite{yang2024hyy33}.\textbf{ Large language models as judges} are known to correlate well with human preference when the judge is strong \cite{zheng2023judge}, and tend to prefer their own generations \cite{panickssery2024}, so a panel of judges from different developers is recommended over relying on a single judge \cite{verga2024}. The reference is \textbf{human evaluation}, which is reported either as five-point rating \cite{qian2023llm} or head-to-head preference \cite{cao2025tooled}.

\section{Review of Existing Research}
\label{sec:2.2}

The work relevant to this thesis can be divided into six groups. The first is the benchmark that originated the field. The second is the family of models that have been trained end to end on that benchmark. The third focuses on the listener's rather than the speaker's perspective. The fourth is the emergence of large language models, altering the definition of a good response. The fifth is a more limited question that is of great importance here: do those models recognise emotion or do they write about it well? The sixth is the clinical work that makes use of the same machinery for screening, but not replying. The last section is the most recent study, which is the closest to ours, and with no discussion of how any of this should be evaluated. 

\subsection{The Benchmark That Created the Field}
\label{sec:2.2.1}

This field was created by the study published by Rashkin et al. in 2019 in ACL, which did two things at once. It made available EmpatheticDialogues and it adopted a framing that all subsequent papers have followed: the listener responded to a description of an emotional situation by the person, and the emotion was not a positive or negative sentiment but was one of 32 fine-grained categories \cite{rashkin2019}. The selection of 32 categories is not random. It is the reason why a top-1 accuracy on this corpus is much lower than accuracy figures on two or six class emotion tasks. It pushes a system to differentiate feelings that a sentiment model would overlap, for example \textit{sad}, \textit{lonely}, \textit{disappointed }and \textit{devastated}.

There were two more decisions in that paper that determined all that followed. One is that the emotion label can be given to the emotion generator, or it can be predicted by the emotion generator model; the paper shows both possibilities. The second is a point of reference for the recognition task: a fastText classifier with an accuracy of approximately 43\% \cite{rashkin2019}. That number is cited frequently, and it's important to remember for our own efforts. This was done on the situation description, the text the crowdworker wrote about the event, not on the first message that the speaker gives the listener. The two texts are different lengths and also more or less explicit, as a description of an event is more open about the feeling than a message written to another person. Any system that has to operate on what a user actually types is solving the more difficult of the two problems and Section 5.2.2 gives our own measurement of that gap.

\subsection{Joint Models Trained End to End}
\label{sec:2.2.2}

A series of \textbf{joint models} were then introduced, all of which introduced a mechanism to a single, newly trained encoder-decoder network trained on the corpus. MoEL (Lin et al., 2019) used a mixture of listeners, with one listener for each emotion, and fused their outputs with a softmax over the detected emotion, to allow the model to respond differently to different emotions \cite{lin2019moel}. MIME (Majumder et al., 2020) began with a remark on human listeners: they reflect the speaker's emotion to some extent and compensate for it to some extent \cite{majumder2020mime}. EmpDG (Li et al., 2020) incorporated an adversarial discriminator that decided if a reply appeared empathetic, as well as emotion signals at greater than one granularity level \cite{li2020empdg}. The next three papers all contributed knowledge that was not in the corpus. KEMP (Li et al., 2022) introduced external commonsense and concept knowledge, enabling the model to make inferences about the situation rather than just the words in the situation \cite{li2022kemp}. CEM (Sabour et al., 2022) took that notion further by making commonsense inferences about the speaker in particular, and eliciting their probable reactions, intentions and needs \cite{sabour2022cem}. CASE (Zhou et al., 2023) aligned a commonsense graph with a concept graph, on the basis that a listener should correlate what the speaker thinks with what the speaker feels, which are two different graphs \cite{zhou2023case}. Emotion-semantic vectors that vary over the course of the conversation were then used by ESCM (Yang et al., 2023) and explicitly supervised on the emotional signal \cite{yang2023escm}.

These papers read sequentially show an incremental increase in emotion accuracy: 30.6\% for EmpDG, 30.8\% for MoEL, 36.6\% for KEMP, 37.8\% for CEM, and 38.9\% for CASE and 41.2\% for ESCM. There are two points to be made here: The improvement is due to the addition of knowledge and structure, not to the way in which the emotion is used after it is predicted; and even after four years of architectural work, the best of these models still misclassifies the speaker's feeling in about three out of five cases. A similar model gives an indication of the underlying reason. Liu et al. (2021) used RoBERTa-GPT2, which used the same task and the same corpus but initialised its encoder from pre-trained RoBERTa and its decoder from pre-trained GPT-2 rather than training them from scratch, and achieved 51.5\% \cite{liu2021robertagpt2} - about ten points higher than the best knowledge-augmented model on the list. The comparison indicates that for a corpus of this size, it was not what was built into the models, but how little text they had seen that was the limiting factor. That's the first reason our own design allocates its parameter budget to strong pre-trained components and how they are connected.

\subsection{The Listener's Side of the Conversation}
\label{sec:2.2.3}

The majority of the above work models the speaker: their feelings, their thoughts, what their situation suggests. A smaller body of work focuses on the listener rather, and it's more useful than the number of citations would imply, for a rule-based design. From a large sample of EmpatheticDialogues, Welivita and Pu (2020) created a taxonomy of empathetic response intents, which revealed that the human listeners in this corpus use a small and describable set of behaviours: questioning, acknowledging, consoling, agreeing, encouraging, sympathising and a few others \cite{welivita2020}. This is important for any rule-writing method, including our own, because it demonstrates that there is structure in supportive replies. If the human listeners are to use a short list of moves, then to get a generator to recognize the feeling, to reply to the situation, and to ask no more than one question is not an arbitrary limitation imposed on the model but a description of what the members of the corpus already do.

There, the broader context of emotion recognition in conversation provides the other half. Poria et al. (2019) review the field and explain why it is not the same task as sentence-level classification: the same words can have different feelings based on what precedes them, and context is not an optional extra, but input to the system \cite{poria2019erc}. This is the justification for considering the number of times a detector has been read as a parameter to be measured, and not assumed, as is reported for our detector in Section 5.3.2.

\subsection{Large Language Models Enter the Task}
\label{sec:2.2.4}

With the advent of \textbf{large language models}, everything has changed, and Qian et al. (2023) is the paper that made that inevitable. They conducted a human evaluation of ChatGPT against the purpose-built models mentioned above, and the raters overwhelmingly favored ChatGPT with a score of 4.64 out of 5 compared to 3.08 for CEM \cite{qian2023llm}. It's not possible to explain that gap with tuning. The paper then continued to explore a path that is relevant to our design: the model's responses are better when the emotion is explicitly provided and better still when the model is instructed to reason about the speaker's situation before responding. That is, the one system that did best in the literature of empathetic-response was a general system to which structure was provided from outside the system. Our two-stage pipeline is how we can provide just that. Loh and Raamkumar (2023) tested large models for counselling against counselling criteria for online mental-health support and came to a similar conclusion, with the caveat that without structure, the responses remain generic \cite{loh2023harnessing}.

The added structure can take a number of different forms; the four recent papers illustrate these. Each has a price tag to consider. TOOL-ED (Cao et al., 2025) treats a commonsense knowledge base as a tool the model may call during generation, under a framework the authors name empathetic knowledge tool calling; the model decides when to consult COMET or CICERO rather than having their output injected into every input, which avoids the noise that unrestricted knowledge introduces, and the authors report that this makes the system independent of any one knowledge base \cite{cao2025tooled}. The same knowledge that is embedded in the architectures of KEMP and CEM, but is now pulled as needed. The cost can be seen in the setup: They fine-tune a 7B model, and the training is reported to be completed on 48 GB GPUs \cite{cao2025tooled}---six times our thesis's GPU memory.

The authors of Wu et al. (2025) state that the same situation should be responded to differently to different persons, and use an MBTI classifier with around 84\% accuracy on its own benchmark, in addition to images alongside the text when conditioning the generation \cite{wu2025traits}. Their model with personality information achieves 45.4\% emotion accuracy, and emotion accuracy drops almost 4 points on the same measure when personality information is removed, a clear proof of the added value of this signal \cite{wu2025traits}. The constraint in the context of our aim is the input, not the output: the design is based on the assumption that the visual data and personality labels are available in a face-to-face scenario, and neither is available to a text-only system reading a single message.

Wang et al. (2025) participated in the NLPCC 2025 emotional support conversation task, in which the task is not just one sentence but a strategy that needs to be followed throughout the conversation, and compared low-rank adaptation with full fine-tuning on the Qwen2.5 models \cite{wang2025esc}. There are two facts that are immediately applicable to this thesis. Their best configuration boosted the combined score of the task from 30.87 (base) to 41.17 and they did not do a full fine tuning, but used low rank adaptation and the rank and the scaling factors of the adapter was shown to make a difference to the score \cite{wang2025esc} and their settings bracket the ones our adapter uses (as stated in Section 4.4.4). The other detail is scale, as the entry fine-tuned models up to 72B parameters and trained on 1,500 dialogues, which is the other end of the resource question from the system built here.

Rasool et al. (2025) focus on psychotherapy transcripts instead of crowdsourced conversations, and inject emotion-aware embeddings at three levels, word, sentence and whole session, into four generators: Flan-T5, Llama 2 13B, DeepSeek-R1 and GPT-4, and use a vector index to retrieve related passages over the embeddings \cite{rasool2025}. The retrieval portion of that design is similar to the one this thesis constructs as a baseline, which relies on sentence embeddings to get information about a material at generation time \cite{reimers2019sbert,lewis2020rag} and is a useful comparison for Section 5.2.5 where our retrieval variant is measured and rejected. The authors report gains in empathy, coherence and fluency compared to their baselines, but also note that their paired significance tests do not meet the traditional threshold and there is no human evaluation in this study \cite{rasool2025}. Lastly, Li et al. (2024) do not follow the knowledge route, but the emotional route, which involves the model deriving its answer step by step with emotion \cite{li2024emotioncot}. Our stage one emotion choice is a condensed version of the same concept: one conscious choice, documented, prior to the writing of any response.

\subsection{Do These Models Recognise Emotion?}
\label{sec:2.2.5}

All of the above is about the quality of the answers. Whether large models reliably \textit{detect }emotion is a different question, and the one the thesis hinges on, since a decoupled design will only have value if a dedicated detector is actually more effective at detecting emotion. The evidence indicates that it is.

Feng et al. (2023) tested large language models for affect recognition in conversation and concluded that they do not necessarily outperform purpose-trained classifiers, but are comparable in performance \cite{feng2023affect}. Belay et al. (2025) solidified the comparison. They created a multi-label emotion dataset in four Ethiopian languages (EthioEmo), introduced the English SemEval-2018 data, and compared encoder-only, encoder-decoder and decoder-only models along with in-context learning, where examples were provided in the prompt. Their conclusion is straightforward: even smaller, more efficient pre-trained language models, when fine-tuned, perform better than the zero-shot performance of large language models on this task, and the best decoder-only they tested averaged about 46 compared to an encoder fine-tuned to about 72 on its best language \cite{belay2025}. They also discover that even for English, correct multi-label classification is still not enough, and that there is a significant difference between well-resourced and poorly resourced languages \cite{belay2025} --- something we come back to in Section 5.6.6 as our system is English only.

Two additional papers explain \textit{why }recognition is difficult, but not \textit{how well} it is accomplished. Koga et al. (2023) investigated emotions that are evoked but not expressed and concluded that a significant portion of the issue is inference, not classification \cite{koga2023implicit}. The other difficulty comes from Belay et al. themselves, in that an utterance often contains more than one emotion at a time \cite{belay2025} -- this is one of the reasons why our detector returns three candidates and not one, and is part of why an utterance has more than one emotion.

The best single figure doesn't come from a benchmark paper, it comes from a deployed system. The Chirpy Cardinal open-domain agent developed for the Alexa Prize contained a RoBERTa classifier on the same 32 EmpatheticDialogues categories and the authors reported an accuracy of approximately 61.5\% on the initial utterance, with errors focused on neighbouring emotions instead of being randomly distributed \cite{paranjape2020chirpy}. Our own results have both components of that finding. The accuracy that we achieve and the same kind of distribution of errors between neighbouring emotions, which render a three-candidate output useful rather than evasive, is reported in Section 5.1.2 and the reason for the difference with our figure is explained in Section 5.5.1.

\subsection{Clinical and Mental-Health Applications}
\label{sec:2.2.6}

The clinical end of the field is using the same machines for screening and not replying, and it is worth looking at, as it will be the standard of care this type of system will be held to. Lan et al. (2024) used large language models for depression detection on social media text \cite{lan2024depression}. The problem was re-phrased in the CLPsych 2025 shared task as the task of tracking mental-health dynamics over a user's timeline instead of classifying a single post \cite{tseriotou2025clpsych}. Kumar et al. (2024) conducted a survey of the two problems that limit the entire region: there is a lack of labelled clinical data, and the bias that arises from training on the little that is available is hard to detect \cite{kumar2024unlocking}.

The most relevant caution for our own design is that from the systematic review by Sorin et al. (2024), which we act on directly. In and of itself, the fact that large language models were often scored as empathetic as clinicians or more so, across the studies it examined, is remarkable. The problem the review notes is not that the people were incompetent, but that they were the same: the empathy was repetitive, the same formulations repeated across answers \cite{sorin2024jmir}. This is a failure that a comparison with the reference cannot detect, because a repetitive response may be very similar to a good response. That's why we have a repetition measure in our own signals in Section 5.1.4, and that is a measure that none of the joint models mentioned above report. 

\subsection{The Closest Prior Work}
\label{sec:2.2.7}

The study most similar to this thesis is the \textbf{Hybrid Empathetic Framework} by Yang et al. (2024). It first adds a small, specialised empathetic model to a large language model, and then predicts the emotion in two steps, along with determining the reason for the emotion \cite{yang2024hef}. That's the same architectural intuition that this thesis seeks to achieve: a small model that is good at emotion feeding a large model that is good at language, with the emotional decision made in a stage of its own, not folded into generation.

The distinctions are in the purpose of each system. Their framework is aimed at benchmark performance on empathetic generation. Instead, ours is designed to operate entirely within 8 GB of memory on a consumer GPU, so that a user's disclosure never leaves the machine; to expose the entire emotional decision, including the candidate emotions, their confidence and the words that produced them, as an audit trail attached to every reply; and to evaluate replies based on what they must do to be a supportive reply, not on how well they match a reference. The existence of that paper means our contribution cannot be, and is not claimed to be, the idea of separating detection from generation. It is what that separation is designed to provide and the proof that is collected for it.

\subsection{How Empathetic Replies Should Be Judged}
\label{sec:2.2.8}

Finally, \textbf{how this is to be measured or judged} has become a literature in itself, and it is the aspect of the old papers upon which this thesis falls most for the question we must ask can only be answered by the measurement of that.

Zheng et al. (2023) determined that strong language models when used as judges align with human preference with over 80\% frequency, comparable to interhuman consensus, and documented the biases shared by these language models, such as the preference for longer answers \cite{zheng2023judge}. Panickssery et al. (2024) pointed out that such evaluators identify and appreciate their own generations and thus the choice of the evaluator is not just for convenience but a methodological decision as the evaluator from the same family of one of the systems under test is not a neutral instrument \cite{panickssery2024}. The remedy of this thesis, which is a combination of multiple judges from different model families, was proposed by Verga et al. (2024), who stated both intra-model bias and lower cost \cite{verga2024}.

The other complementary solution is to train a model directly on human empathy rating, without asking it to mimic a rater \cite{giorgi2024wassa} which is the case of the hyy33 system used here \cite{yang2024hyy33}. Its usefulness in this context is that it is unrelated to any system being evaluated and thus would not favour its own output. The human evaluation is still the benchmark, and it has been recently reported as a rating on the 5-point scale \cite{qian2023llm} or as a head to head comparison between two replies \cite{cao2025tooled}. In this thesis, all three types of evidence are employed -- the model judges and the trained classifier in the same panel -- as described in Section 5.1.5, and standardized before being averaged for this reason.

\section{Summary of Key Findings}
\label{sec:2.3}

The above review of the literature yields seven findings. Each one is expressed with the evidence used to support it, and what this thesis does with it, as it together creates the space this work inhabits, and the form of the system constructed in Chapter 4.

\textbf{The pre-training is more important than the architecture for this task:} the joint models accumulate improvements over 4 years, ranging from 30.6\% to 41.2\% emotion accuracy with the addition of mixtures of listeners, emotion mimicry, adversarial training, commonsense knowledge, graph alignment and dynamic emotion vectors \cite{lin2019moel,majumder2020mime,li2020empdg,li2022kemp,sabour2022cem,zhou2023case,yang2023escm} and RoBERTa-GPT2 gets 51.5\% on the same task just by using pre-trained components instead of training from scratch \cite{liu2021robertagpt2}. There is a difference of ten points between the two methods, the difference being the cheaper wins. The key takeaway is that it is better to invest the parameter budget in a good pre-trained model, and in how it's connected, than on a machine built on a set of 25,000 conversations.

\textbf{Large language models produce better replies than purpose-built models of empathy, but do not reliably detect emotion: }Human raters prefer ChatGPT over CEM by 4.64 compared to 3.08 out of 5 \cite{qian2023llm} and a systematic review of models shows that large models are rated at par with or better than clinician models of empathy \cite{sorin2024jmir}. However, these models have been found to perform no better than trained classifiers in conversational affect \cite{feng2023affect} and worse than fine-tuned encoders in multi-label emotion \cite{belay2025} and even difficult to compete with in implied emotion \cite{koga2023implicit}. This asymmetry (strong generation and not-so-good recognition) is the direct argument for recognizing a separate model and having the generator do only what it is good at.

\textbf{A small fine-tuned model can beat a large prompted one at classification: }This is the sharpest piece of evidence for our architecture, and it comes from outside the empathy literature. Belay et al. evaluated the performance of different models (encoder-only, encoder-decoder, and decoder-only) on the same multi-label emotion dataset, and demonstrated that fine-tuning smaller and more efficient pre-trained models still achieves better results than the zero-shot performance of large language models \cite{belay2025}. Our own corpus has a corresponding figure, a RoBERTa classifier with the 32 EmpatheticDialogues emotions with around 61.5\% \cite{paranjape2020chirpy}. A detector with a few hundred million parameters is thus not a hardware compromise but on this task it is a better instrument.

\textbf{Machine empathy is repetitive and hardly anyone measures it: }The inherent weakness of systematic review is that it is not incompetence, but sameness: fluent, kind and from a small pool of phrases \cite{sorin2024jmir}. There is no repetition measure in any of the joint models considered above, and it is not possible to identify the problem using the reference-based comparison, because the repeated reply may be similar to the reference reply. This is why we have a rep measure in our own signals, and why it is reported for all systems, not just our own.

\textbf{Structure helps, and there are multiple ways of providing it, each with a cost: }Telling the model the emotion, or asking it to reason before it answers, \cite{qian2023llm,li2024emotioncot}; providing a knowledge base to call upon as a tool, \cite{cao2025tooled}; conditioning on inferred personality and visual input, \cite{wu2025traits}; fine tuning with a low rank adapter, \cite{wang2025esc}; or retrieving related passages at generation time, \cite{lewis2020rag,rasool2025}: all of these add structure to an otherwise unconstrained generator and all report gains. What they all lack is what is worth its cost under a hardware constraint: 48 GB of GPU memory for tool calling \cite{cao2025tooled} and models up to 72B parameters for the fine-tuned approach \cite{wang2025esc} and multimodal input with personality labels for the trait-aware approach \cite{wu2025traits}. Chapter 5 does just that within 8 GB, as well as the retrieval variant, which we measure and dismiss.

\textbf{The weakest aspect of the field is evaluation, and there are workable alternatives now available: }Published comparisons rely on overlap with a single reference reply and can't determine if a reply acknowledged the feeling or asked five questions in a row, and one recent study reports improvements that fail to reach the conventional level of significance and that no human rated \cite{rasool2025}. The options that are now available include a classifier trained directly on human empathy ratings \cite{giorgi2024wassa,yang2024hyy33} and panels of language-model judges from different developers, which mitigate the self-preference that any given judge exhibits \cite{zheng2023judge,verga2024,panickssery2024}. These enable this thesis to be judged by what their replies \textit{do}, and not by what they look like, and to test statistically every difference.

\textbf{The separation of the small model from the large one has been proposed but for another purpose: }The Hybrid Empathetic Framework first separates a small empathetic model from the large model, and then predicts the emotion in two steps \cite{yang2024hef}. Our work has that structure and differs in its purpose: a full system within 8 GB of consumer memory, an emotion exposed as an auditable record, and not an internal step, an evaluation based on rule adherence, an empathy panel and paired statistics.

\textbf{The gap this thesis fills:} To our knowledge, no existing work builds an empathetic response system that (i) runs completely in consumer hardware, so that none of the information about a user's disclosure ever leaves the machine, (ii) exposes every facet of its emotional decision, including the candidate emotions, their confidence, and the words that manifested them, for inspection before generating a reply, and (iii) is evaluated against single-model baselines of both comparable and larger size, on measures that correspond to what a supportive reply must do, with every difference tested statistically. Chapters 4 and 5 develop and test just that system.
