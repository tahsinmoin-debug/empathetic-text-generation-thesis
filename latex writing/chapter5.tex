\section{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:5.1}

Every design built in Chapter 4 answered the same conversations and was evaluated on the same five signals. Section 5.2 explains why they differ and Section 5.4 says which differences are large enough to trust.

\subsection{Emotion Detection}
\label{sec:5.1.2}

The detector reaches \textbf{50.3\% top-1, 76.5\% top-3 and 84.3\% top-5 }accuracy over 32 emotions, with a macro-F1 of 49.4\% against a chance level of 3.1\%. Performance varies widely: \textit{caring} (F1 70.1\%), \textit{lonely }(69.5\%) and \textit{jealous} (64.7\%) are recognised well, while\textit{ angry }(18.2\%),\textit{ ashamed }(22.2\%) and\textit{ joyful} (30.9\%) are hardest, a 52-point range with 7 of the 32 emotions below 40\%. Macro-F1 is reported because it weights every emotion equally. The test partition is close to balanced (50 to 127 conversations per emotion) and accuracy and weighted-F1 differ by 0.9 points, so the result is not produced by the commoner emotions.

The shape of the errors matters more than their number. All twelve of the most frequent mistakes are near neighbours: \textit{sentimental = nostalgic, afraid = terrified, angry = furious, sad = devastated}, differences of intensity inside one family of feeling rather than confusions between unrelated emotions, as reported for a comparable classifier \cite{paranjape2020chirpy}. Two consequences shaped the design: a reply built on the wrong label is rarely built on the wrong \textit{kind} of feeling, and the correct label sits in the top three far more often than at the top, which is why three candidates are passed to the generator.

\subsection{Response system analysis}
\label{sec:5.1.3}

The mechanically checkable measures are in Table~\ref{tab:5.1}; how empathetic the replies are is assessed in Section 5.1.5.

\textbf{Compliance with the rules:} the final system acknowledges the feeling, asks one question and stays within 120 words in\textbf{ 60.0\%} of replies, below the largest single model (82.3\%), its own generator prompted alone (73.7\%) and top-1 prompting (65.3\%), and above the same-size single model (51.3\%). Two rows need care. The fine-tuned model reproduces the style it learned rather than the behaviour the rules ask for, at 10 words a reply and 30.0\% acknowledgement, and the human replies it trained on satisfy all three rules in only 4.3\% of cases. Gemma-3-4B's replies are not bad, but at 133 words it simply ignores the length limit (Section 5.2.3).

\textbf{Emotional fidelity:} a well-formed reply may still answer the wrong feeling, so each reply is read back by the detector and compared with the conversation's label. The final system leads all twelve systems at \textbf{36.7\%}, ahead of the largest single model (32.3\%), the same-size single model (25.7\%) and Phi-4-mini prompted alone (28.3\%).

\subsection{Inside the Two-Stage Decision, and Repetition}
\label{sec:5.1.4}

The detector's three candidates contained the gold emotion in 78.0\% of conversations, and the generator overrode the detector's first choice in 40.7\% of them. The stage-1 choice matches the gold label in \textbf{42.3\%} of conversations against the detector's 50.3\% (Section 5.4.3), so the two-stage design does not earn its gains by naming the gold label more often; Section 5.6.2 explains how it does.

The commonest complaint about machine empathy is repetition \cite{sorin2024jmir}, measured here as the share of replies containing one of that system's ten most frequent five-word phrases. The final system's rate is 58.7\%, with "how are you feeling right" in 49 replies, against 77.0\% for the same generator prompted generically: per-emotion prompts reduce repetition but do not cure it.

\subsection{The Empathy Panel}
\label{sec:5.1.5}

The\textbf{ two judges} had to meet three requirements: flagship size, since only strong judges track human preference reliably \cite{zheng2023judge}; no shared family with anything they judge \cite{panickssery2024}; and membership of a panel drawn from different developers, which carries less bias than any single judge \cite{verga2024}. Nemotron 3 Ultra (NVIDIA) and gpt-oss-120b (OpenAI) meet all three, where Phi-4-mini (Microsoft) and Qwen (Alibaba) do not. Each scores empathy, relevance and safety from 1 to 10.

\textbf{The third rater is not a language model at all,} which is why it is on the panel: the hyy33 system from the WASSA 2024 shared task \cite{yang2024hyy33,giorgi2024wassa} is a transformer trained on conversation turns that people rated for empathy, returning one score from 0 to 10. It is unrelated to every model compared here and does not share the length preference of large models (Section 5.4.4).

\textbf{Combining them:} the raters do not share a range, the judges spanning 3.9 to 8.2 and the classifier only 3.4 to 5.8, so each is standardised across the eight judged systems before the three are averaged equally. Weighting the classifier at 50\% rather than 33\% changes almost nothing (rank agreement 0.98), and nothing is tuned.

The single model with 7.6 billion parameters leads (+1.11), followed by\textbf{ the final system (+0.59)}, the same generator alone (+0.53), top-1 prompting with it (+0.51), the same-size single model (+0.15), the initial pipeline (+0.05), zero-shot prompting (-0.72) and the fine-tuned model (-2.22). Ours is first among the systems that fit requirement N1, \textbf{the 8 GB budget}. The two-stage design and the same generator alone swap places depending on the rater: the model judges place the latter slightly ahead on empathy (8.04 against 7.84), the classifier places ours clearly ahead (5.05 against 4.75), and combined ours scores higher. The sample is small and we claim no more than this (Section 5.4.5): a decoupled design does not cost empathy.

\textbf{How we established that the scoring was fair:} Figure~\ref{fig:5.1} shows the prompt each judge receives and the five controls around it. The first matters most: a judge sees only the user's message and one reply, never the system's name. The raters are then checked against each other rather than trusted: the two judges agree reply by reply at rho = 0.69 and rank the systems almost identically, and the independent classifier agrees on the ordering at rho = 0.74 (Section 5.4.4).

\textbf{All emotions were assessed, with no favourable selection:} the sample holds the shortest and longest message for each of the 32 emotions, from 4 to 54 words. The final system scores above the panel mean of 7.84 on the emotions of acute distress and below it on positive or settled states, which with two conversations behind each value is indicative only.

\textbf{The four leading systems do not differ significantly} on the judges' own scores after correction (Section 5.4.5); all four use a generator of 3.8 billion parameters or more and their panel scores fall within 0.08 of one another. What the panel separates cleanly is scale: every system below them has 1.5 billion parameters or fewer. Section 5.5.5 provides the comparison that settles the question.

\subsection{Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:5.1.6}

A rating study checked the automatic measures against people. Participants read 8 situations and, for each, three replies in counterbalanced order, from the final system, the same-size single model and the largest single model, rating each from 1 to 5 on three scales with written anchors: empathy and emotional validation, contextual relevance, and supportiveness. Each situation ends with a preference question, which keeps the head-to-head comparison used in similar studies \cite{cao2025tooled} beside the ratings. Five of the eight situations were also scored by the AI judges, which lets the two rankings be compared.

Forty people took part, all consented, and none were excluded, giving 2,880 ratings and 320 preference choices. They were not told that any system was ours.

This is the clearest result in the thesis. The final system is rated highest on all three scales and preferred in all eight situations. It scores 4.16 out of 5 on recognising and validating the feeling, against 3.69 for a single model almost twice its size and 3.52 for one of its own class; 4.07 on addressing the specific situation, against 3.60 and 3.48; and 4.09 on how supported a person would feel, against 3.59 and 3.44. Asked which reply responds best, participants chose it 196 times out of 320, a rate of 61.3\% where chance is 33.3\%, against 21.6\% and 17.2\%. Every one of these differences survives correction (Section 5.4.6).

Two things deserve stating plainly. People placed our system above the 7-billion-parameter single model that the empathy panel had placed above it, the judges agreeing with them on the five situations both assessed. And agreement between individual participants is low, so the finding rests on the aggregate of forty people rather than on any one of them.

\section{Analysis of Design Solutions}
\label{sec:5.2}

This section takes one design question at a time, and closes by comparing the alternatives on criteria that performance figures do not capture.

\subsection{Decoupled against Monolithic Designs}
\label{sec:5.2.1}

Four designs answer the same conversations without a detector, and they fail in three ways. \textbf{A single model learns the style of its training data, not the behaviour we want:} the LoRA-tuned generator writes short, casual replies that rarely name the feeling and comes last on empathy, fidelity and the judges. \textbf{With no emotion at all, prompting leaves nothing to work from: }zero-shot prompting asks the one required question in 22.7\% of replies. \textbf{Folding both jobs into one model closes part of the gap but not the part that matters:} the same-size single model reaches 51.3\% on all rules while staying barely more faithful to the real emotion than zero-shot prompting (25.7\% against 24.7\%).

Because that comparison changes the generator as well as the design, we built the test that changes only the design: our own generator with the single-model prompt and no detector. It follows the rules more often than the decoupled system (73.7\% against 60.0\%), but its empathy and fidelity match the smaller single model (4.75 and 27.0\%) and rise to 5.05 and 36.7\% once the detector and guidance are added (Section 5.5.5). \textbf{A larger single model is still better overall: }Qwen2.5-7B leads everywhere except on structure, where it shares the same-size model's prompt.

\subsection{Detector Alternatives}
\label{sec:5.2.2}

Two findings settled the detector. First, \textbf{what the detector is trained on matters more than which network is used.} Training GPT-2 Medium on situation descriptions while deploying it on the user's own message cost about nine points of accuracy (56.9\% against 48.2\%); retraining on the text the model actually receives was the single largest improvement made.

Second, \textbf{among encoders trained on the right input, models of the same size are indistinguishable on top-1 accuracy }(48.8-50.2\%); they separate only on ranking quality, RoBERTa-base reaching 85.0\% top-5 against 81.7-82.7\% for BERT and ELECTRA (Figure~\ref{fig:5.5}). Ranking is exactly what the two-stage design consumes. RoBERTa-large added 2.9 points of top-1 accuracy for 7.4 times the training cost and changed no measure of reply quality (Section 5.4.3), so RoBERTa-base was kept.

\subsection{Generator Alternatives}
\label{sec:5.2.3}

The generator decides how well the rules are followed: Phi-4-mini reaches 65.3\% adherence, Qwen2.5-1.5B 57.7\% and Gemma-3-4B 22.0\%. Gemma looks the most empathetic of the three, scoring higher than Phi-4-mini on the empathy classifier and acknowledging a feeling in 96.7\% of replies. But it averages 133 words, 72\% of its replies exceed the limit, and every classifier we measured rewards length (Section 5.4.4). Comparing replies of similar length removes the advantage: Phi-4-mini matches or beats Gemma in any band where both reply, and Gemma leads only above 120 words, where Phi-4-mini does not reply at all.

\subsection{Integration Alternatives}
\label{sec:5.2.4}

How the two models are connected is a design decision in its own right, and the variants differ in how much reasoning they ask of the generator. With the same detector and generator, the two-stage design raises emotional fidelity from 28.3\% to 36.7\% and empathy from 4.89 to 5.05, both reliable after correction, and shortens replies from 80.4 to 57.1 words, at an unreliable cost of 5.3 points of rule adherence.

The benefit depends on the generator's capacity. The same change applied to Qwen2.5-1.5B drops adherence to 36.7\% and acknowledgement to 47.0\%: a small model given more to reason about becomes less compliant with the output format. One limitation should be explicit: this comparison removes the top-3 selection, the per-emotion guidance and the attention cues at once, and separating them is listed as future work (Section 5.6.6).

\subsection{Retrieval Augmentation}
\label{sec:5.2.5}

\textbf{How retrieval is used:} every training conversation was embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 \cite{reimers2019sbert}. At generation time the detector's emotion filtered the pool, the two most similar conversations were retrieved by cosine similarity, and the human replies were pasted into the system prompt as worked examples above the same three rules \cite{lewis2020rag}. A second attempt restricted the pool to replies containing a single question.

\textbf{It reduced rule adherence in both versions:} 54.3\% and 43.7\% against 63.7\% without retrieval. The explanation is in the data: only 4.3\% of real listener replies satisfy all three rules, so what is retrieved is a style that contradicts the instructions in the same prompt. Acknowledgement fell from 70.3\% to 57.0\%.

\textbf{Nothing was gained in exchange:} empathy 4.85 against 4.80 without retrieval and emotional fidelity 27.7\% against 26.0\%, both far smaller than the uncertainty on those estimates (Section 5.4.2). Three cells of the first attempt are unrecoverable, its outputs having been overwritten before the measures were computed. Showing a model how people really reply teaches it to break the rules the same prompt imposes, not to keep them.

\subsection{Assessment against Criteria beyond Performance}
\label{sec:5.2.6}

Performance is not the only criterion. Every single-model design, at any size, keeps its emotional decision inside itself: there is nothing to inspect and nowhere to place a safeguard. The decoupled designs expose it, and the selected one exposes the most: three candidates, the chosen emotion and the words behind it, with the detector, the guidance and the generator each replaceable on its own.

The performance measures disagree. On what the replies do the final system is mid-table, following all three rules less often than the largest single model or its own generator alone, while expressing the person's real emotion more often than any other system. On how empathetic the replies are judged to be it is second of eight, beaten only by a single model with 1.9 times its parameters and 62\% more GPU memory, and first among the systems meeting requirement N1 of Section 3.1.2.

\section{Final Design Adjustments}
\label{sec:5.3}

Testing the early pipeline, a GPT-2 Medium detector with a Qwen2.5-1.5B generator, exposed several flaws. Each adjustment below followed a measured result and was re-evaluated on the same 300 conversations.

\subsection{Adjustments to the Detector, the Generator and the Prompt}
\label{sec:5.3.1}

Measuring the first detector on the input it actually receives revealed a mismatch: it had been trained on situation descriptions, which name the feeling almost explicitly, while the pipeline hands it the user's conversational message. It was retrained on first messages and, following Section 5.2.2, GPT-2 Medium was replaced by RoBERTa-base.

Three comparisons from Section 5.2 were kept after re-measurement. \textbf{The generator was replaced:} Phi-4-mini followed the rules more often than Qwen2.5-1.5B, so d2 became Phi-4-mini in 4-bit.\textbf{ The emotion decision was given its own call: }the correct emotion is among the detector's top three in 76.5\% of conversations while the top label is wrong about half the time, and an earlier version that decided and replied in one call damaged rule-following. \textbf{Guidance was written for each of the 32 emotions} in place of the generic "the user is feeling X", because answering loneliness is not the same as answering pride. Together, with detector and generator fixed, these raised emotional fidelity from 28.3\% to 36.7\% and empathy from 4.89 to 5.05, both reliable after correction, at an unreliable 5.3-point cost in rule adherence, and shortened replies from 80.4 to 57.1 words.

The original design specified an attention mechanism but never implemented one: the library's default returns no weights and raises no error, so the feature would have been silently inactive. The adjustment selects an implementation that returns weights, stops if they are missing, merges fragments back into whole words, and passes only the highest-scoring 40\% of tokens as at most three phrases.

\subsection{From Single Replies to a Conversation}
\label{sec:5.3.2}

How much of a conversation the detector should read, once the system becomes the agent of Section 4.2.7, had to be measured.

\textbf{Why the dataset cannot answer it: }conversations in EmpatheticDialogues run to at most 4 turns, with a median of 2, and the gold emotion never changes within one, so there is no multi-turn emotional shift to tune a window against. We therefore wrote conversations containing the changes a real support conversation produces, above all the "I'm fine, actually I'm not" pattern. This is illustrative evidence for a design choice rather than statistical evidence, and \textbf{the listener's reading in the table is our own judgement.}

\textbf{Across 10 short conversations }with a deliberate shift, widening from one turn to four moved the prediction to what a listener would hear in five, left it unchanged in three, and in two exchanged one neighbour of the intended emotion for another; in none did a correct reading become wrong. The clearest case is the one that prompted the test: "I am okay. Well, not properly" reads as \textit{content} at 72\% alone, and as \textit{angry} once the previous turn, "Someone yelled at me today very badly", is included.

\textbf{But a window can also be too wide:} Table~\ref{tab:5.4} widens it on four long conversations of six to eight turns, and four is the widest setting that degrades none of them. At six turns the break-up conversation, correct at four turns (\textit{lonely}, 61\%), becomes \textit{guilty} at 64\% once the anger of earlier turns re-enters the window. The control stays \textit{anxious} at every width, so the damage comes from stale emotions competing with the current one, not from input length. Neither setting recovers the burnout conversation, which we report rather than omit.

\textbf{The fixed four-turn window was kept:} widening it only when the detector was unsure, using the median confidence of 0.632 as the threshold, gave the same answer in all ten scenarios.

\subsection{Adjustments That Were Rejected or Deferred}
\label{sec:5.3.3}

Not every candidate change passed. Retrieval augmentation cut rule adherence from 63.7\% to 43.7\% and was rejected (Section 5.2.5), and a larger detector improved no measure of reply quality inside the pipeline despite being 2.9 points more accurate alone (Section 5.4.3).

Two changes were \textbf{postponed.} A repetition penalty would probably reduce the 58.7\% boilerplate rate, but regenerating the replies would have invalidated the judge scores and human-evaluation items already built on them. A pre-generation crisis filter, the first item of future work, must be installed before any public deployment.

\textbf{Fine-tuned baseline:} the monolithic LoRA baseline carried a configuration error, since corrected. It averaged 200.7 words and 7.07 questions per reply because 99\% of its training answers were padded with the end-of-turn token and the collator removed padding from the loss, so it was never trained to stop. It was retrained with the model's own padding token, and a start-up check now aborts training if end-of-turn padding would be masked. The corrected model ends its replies normally at 10.0 words and follows all three rules in 22.3\% of cases; every result in this thesis refers to it.

\section{Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:5.4}

This section separates the differences reported so far that are large enough to build on from those that are not.

\subsection{How the Tests Were Done}
\label{sec:5.4.1}

Every system answers all 300 conversations and every detector is tested on all 2,542, so all comparisons are paired. For binary outcomes, such as whether a reply followed all the rules, we use McNemar's exact test; for scores, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which assumes nothing about the shape of the distribution. Each comparison also carries a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval from 10,000 resamples with seed 42, so the size of a difference is visible and not only its direction.

The final system is compared with seven baselines on each measure, so p-values are adjusted within each measure by the Holm-Bonferroni procedure. \textbf{A difference is considered real if the adjusted p-value is < 0.05 and the confidence interval does not contain zero.} Independent group comparisons use Fisher's exact test and the Mann-Whitney U test instead.

\subsection{The Final System against Each Baseline}
\label{sec:5.4.2}

Figure~\ref{fig:5.6} gathers every comparison, coloured by whether it survives correction. Three patterns run through it.

\textbf{Empathy and emotional fidelity improve reliably over every comparable design.} The empathy score exceeds all baselines except the largest single model by 0.16 to 0.25 points, and\textbf{ by 0.30 points over the same generator alone }(95\% CI [+0.19, +0.41], adjusted p < 0.001). Emotional fidelity likewise exceeds every baseline except the largest single model, by 8.3 to 18.3 points, all adjusted p < 0.013, and not because of generator size (Section 5.5.5).

\textbf{We do not claim the rule-adherence difference against the same-size single model }(raw p = 0.032, Holm-corrected p = 0.095). The final system is reliably better than zero-shot prompting, the fine-tuned model and the retrieval pipeline, no different from the initial pipeline or top-1 prompting, and reliably worse than its own generator alone (-13.7, p = 0.002) and the largest single model (-22.3).

\textbf{The one baseline genuinely better on both rules and empathy }is Qwen2.5-7B, leading on rule adherence (+22.3 points), acknowledgement (+16.7), the one-question rule (+6.3) and empathy (+0.71).

\subsection{Is the Detector the Bottleneck?}
\label{sec:5.4.3}

If reply quality depended on detection accuracy, a better detector would produce better replies. Three tests say it does not. \textbf{Replies built on a wrong label are no worse:} splitting each pipeline's replies by whether the detector matched the gold label shows no reliable loss of adherence, 61.2\% against 66.0\% for the initial pipeline (p = 0.40) and none for the final system (p = 0.096); empathy is unaffected too (4.83 against 4.78, p = 0.72). \textbf{Replacing GPT-2 with the more accurate RoBERTa detector changes nothing downstream: }emotional fidelity (p = 0.91) and rule adherence (p = 0.09) do not move, although the two differ significantly as classifiers. \textbf{Doubling the detector makes no difference either:} substituting RoBERTa-large and regenerating all 300 replies made the detector 3.0 points more accurate while leaving rule adherence (p = 0.60), empathy (p = 0.85), emotional fidelity (p = 0.85) and the one-question rule (p = 0.60) unchanged.

The stage-1 choice also matches the gold label in 42.3\% of conversations against the detector's 50.3\% (CI [-13.3, -2.7], p = 0.005), so the two-stage design's advantage does not come from better emotion selection (Section 5.6.2). Detection accuracy is not what limits reply quality, which is why the memory budget is better spent on the generator.

\subsection{What the Empathy Classifier Is Worth}
\label{sec:5.4.4}

Spearman correlations between reply length and classifier score run from 0.14 to 0.54 (p < 0.02): longer replies are more likely to be scored as empathetic, as reported for language-model judges \cite{zheng2023judge}. This cuts both ways. It explains Gemma-3-4B's high score (Section 5.2.3), and it strengthens the case against top-1 prompting, since ours earns the higher score with shorter replies (57.1 against 80.4 words).

Because several conclusions lean on this classifier, we checked it against the judges on the 512 replies both scored. Across systems it tracks them well (rho = 0.74), disagreeing on one. Across individual replies the agreement is weak but genuine (rho = 0.30, p < 0.001) and survives controlling for length (partial rho = 0.25). The classifier is therefore used only to compare system averages, never a single reply, and always beside a second measure.

\subsection{What the Judges Separate, and How Much They Agree}
\label{sec:5.4.5}

Against the same-size single model the final system is rated reliably higher on empathy (+0.70, CI [+0.39, +1.01], p < 0.001), relevance (+0.44, p = 0.023) and overall (+0.44, p = 0.001), and reliably higher overall than the initial pipeline (+0.65), zero-shot prompting (+1.18) and the fine-tuned model (+2.73), all p < 0.001. Against the largest single model it is lower on empathy (-0.30) and overall (-0.21), but neither difference survives correction (p = 0.073 and p = 0.109). \textbf{The judges do not separate the three systems that share a generator at all: }top-1 prompting (overall +0.04, p = 0.71) and that generator alone (overall -0.10, p = 0.16). The two judges correlate at rho = 0.69 on empathy and 0.68 overall, and their rankings are identical but for one pair differing by 0.03.

\subsection{Human-Evaluation Statistics}
\label{sec:5.4.6}

Each participant rated all three systems in all eight situations, so every comparison is paired within a participant and differences in how people use the scale cannot by themselves produce an effect. The tests are Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on per-participant mean ratings, with Holm correction across the nine tests formed by three system pairs and three scales, and 10,000-sample bootstrap intervals.

All six comparisons involving the final system survive correction, and no confidence interval contains zero. Against the same-size single model it gains 0.64 points on empathy (95\% CI +0.41 to +0.88, adjusted p = 0.0001), 0.60 on relevance (+0.33 to +0.88, p = 0.0017) and 0.65 on supportiveness (+0.41 to +0.89, p = 0.0002). Against the larger single model it gains 0.46 on empathy (+0.25 to +0.69, p = 0.0015), 0.47 on relevance (+0.25 to +0.71, p = 0.0039) and 0.50 on supportiveness (+0.29 to +0.72, p = 0.0008). After correction the two single models cannot be told apart on any scale, so the larger model's extra parameters buy no measurable gain.

On the preference question the final system was chosen 196 times out of 320, a rate of 61.3\% (95\% interval 55.7\% to 66.6\%), which a two-sided binomial test places far from the chance rate of one third (p < 0.001), against 21.6\% for the larger model and 17.2\% for the same-size one. The result does not rest on a few situations or people: ours was preferred in all eight situations, by 18 to 29 of the 40 participants, and 22 of the 40 gave it their own highest average rating, against 9 each for the baselines.

Agreement between participants is low, and this is reported rather than set aside. The intraclass correlation is 0.084 for empathy, 0.075 for relevance and 0.082 for supportiveness, and Fleiss' kappa on the preference question is 0.018. Any one person's rating of any one reply therefore carries little information, and the preference is a consistent mild majority in every situation rather than a strong consensus in any. What the study supports is the aggregate.

Human ratings and the judges agree where they overlap. Over the 15 system-situation pairs the judges also scored, the two correlate at a Spearman rho of 0.516 (p = 0.049), and both rank the three systems identically, ours first. On those five situations the judges score ours 8.7 and the larger model 8.6, so the panel's overall preference for the larger model (Section 5.1.5) comes from the wider judged set and not from the situations people saw.

\subsection{Limits of the Analysis}
\label{sec:5.4.7}

Three limits bound all of the above. The 300-conversation sample detects differences of a few percentage points but not smaller ones, and the 64-conversation judge sample is weaker. Emotional fidelity relies on our own detector reading text it has never seen. Holm correction was applied within each measure only, treating the seven baselines as one family.

\section{Comparisons and Relationships}
\label{sec:5.5}

This section compares the results with published work where a comparison is fair.

\subsection{Compared with Published Work}
\label{sec:5.5.1}

Table~\ref{tab:5.5} places our detector among published results on the same 32 emotions, which differ in what each system reads and in which partition the number comes from.

Our detector's 50.3\% exceeds eleven of the published figures: every joint recognition-and-generation model, at 30.6-41.2\% \cite{lin2019moel,majumder2020mime,sabour2022cem,zhou2023case}, the personality-aware models at 45.4-48.3\% \cite{wu2025traits}, and both classifiers from the dataset's own paper, fastText at 43.0\% and DeepMoji at 48.0\%. Four things explain the gap, and only the last is a decision we can take credit for.

\begin{itemize}
\item\textbf{It starts from a far stronger encoder:} ours is fine-tuned from RoBERTa, pre-trained on about 160 GB of text, whereas MoEL, MIME, CEM and CASE build their word representations from GloVe vectors and train on EmpatheticDialogues alone.

\item\textbf{It does one job:} the joint models learn recognition as an auxiliary task, sharing capacity with generation, while ours is trained and selected for classification alone.

\item\textbf{It is selected on the measure it is used for: }the candidate chosen has the best top-5 accuracy, because the design consumes a ranking rather than a single label.

\item\textbf{It is trained on the text it will actually be given: }training on the situation description reads as a better number and is a worse detector, the same model losing about nine points when moved to the message a user really writes (Section 5.3.1).
\end{itemize}

It reaches this accuracy while reading less, one message rather than a whole conversation, which is what a live system has.

Published \textit{generation} scores cannot be compared with ours: distinct-n is reported over a much larger test set and falls as the sample grows, from 32.3\% at 50 replies to 15.6\% at 300 on our own output, while published BERTScore values compare a reply with a reference answer after a multi-turn context. We therefore report measures published work does not: rule adherence, an independent empathy classifier, emotional fidelity and paired significance tests.

Human evaluation allows a comparison of direction. People in published studies strongly prefer LLM replies to those of purpose-built empathetic models, rating ChatGPT at 4.64 out of 5 against 3.08 for CEM \cite{qian2023llm}. Ours points the same way and adds a step: published work shows a large general model preferred to a small purpose-built one, while our 40 participants preferred a small general model with a separate detector to a larger general model without one. Because the protocols differ, only the direction is comparable.

\subsection{What Detector Accuracy and Generator Capacity Each Buy}
\label{sec:5.5.2}

Across the three tests of Section 5.4.3, gains of two to three points in detection accuracy never reached the user. The reason lies in the shape of the errors: an answer written for\textit{ terrified} when the person was \textit{afraid }is still an answer to fear. What is done with the emotion matters more than how accurately it is found: with detector and generator unchanged, per-emotion two-stage prompting raised emotional fidelity by 8.3 points, more than any detector upgrade we tested.

Richer prompts help only a generator with the capacity to follow them. The same additions that leave Phi-4-mini's rule adherence unchanged lower Qwen2.5-1.5B's from 57.7\% to 36.7\% (Section 5.2.4), and adherence under a plain single-model prompt climbs from 51.3\% at 1.5 billion parameters to 82.3\% at 7.6 billion. Instruction complexity should therefore be matched to generator capacity.

\subsection{Cost: Parameters, Memory and Time}
\label{sec:5.5.3}

Parameters alone do not decide what a system costs to run, because the final system loads two models and calls the generator twice per reply. We therefore measured peak GPU memory and response time for it and both single models on the same 50 conversations, on the RTX 4060 used throughout.

At 52.0\% of the Qwen2.5-7B single model's parameters, the final system reaches \textbf{97.5\%} \textbf{of its overall judge score}, keeps 87.7\% of its empathy-classifier score and 72.9\% of its rule adherence, and exceeds it on emotional fidelity. It does this with \textbf{38\% less peak GPU memory} and no reliable difference in response time (CI [-0.03, +0.56] s, p = 0.10); per generated token the two are practically identical (65.7 ms against 65.4 ms, p = 0.91). The extra steps are cheap: detection and cue extraction take 0.016 s and the stage-1 call 0.29 s, about 7\% of the time per reply.

Two qualifications are owed. The Qwen2.5-1.5B single model is lighter at 2.90 GB and 2.6 times faster, so the final system is efficient relative to the large model whose quality it approaches, not to the smallest baseline. And the measurements come from one GPU over 50 messages.

\subsection{Do the Response Rules Measure Something That Matters?}
\label{sec:5.5.4}

Rule adherence is a headline measure, so the rules themselves need justifying. Each effect below is computed within each system, so it cannot arise from differences between systems.

\textbf{Acknowledging the feeling} is the \textit{emotional reactions} mechanism of EPITOME, a framework grounded in established empathy scales \cite{sharma2020epitome}. Within the same system, replies that acknowledge score 0.36 points higher on the empathy classifier (p < 0.001, 3,300 replies); the judges point the same way but not reliably.

\textbf{A single question balances two failures: }a reply with no question at all, and a series of them, which counselling guidance warns against \cite{samhsa2019tip35} and which only 4.7\% of the dataset's listener replies commit. \textbf{No automatic measure rewards this rule,} so we offer it as a usability and safety requirement, not as a route to a higher score.

\textbf{Keeping a reply within 120 words} makes it easy to read while distressed. Here the two measures disagree: the judges rate shorter replies 0.65 points higher on empathy (p = 0.006), while the classifier rates them 0.22 points lower, its known length preference. We follow the judges, who read the text. Two of the three rules are therefore supported by published practice and our own data, and the third by practice alone.

\subsection{What the Detector Itself Contributes}
\label{sec:5.5.5}

The test that isolates the design gives the final system's own generator the single-model prompt and no detector at all. Figure~\ref{fig:5.7} shows the result measure by measure.

\textbf{The gains belong to the design, not to the generator: }moving from Qwen2.5-1.5B to Phi-4-mini as a single model raises rule adherence sharply (51.3\% to 73.7\%, p < 0.001) but not empathy (4.84 to 4.75, p = 0.18) or emotional fidelity (25.7\% to 27.0\%, p = 0.70). Adding the three candidates and the per-emotion guidance to that same generator raises empathy to 5.05 (p < 0.001) and fidelity to 36.7\% (p = 0.007), the empathy gain growing under length control while the fidelity gain shrinks under it (p = 0.13).

\textbf{The price is rule adherence: }with the generator fixed, the decoupled system follows all three rules less often (60.0\% against 73.7\%, p = 0.002), mostly through acknowledgement. The cause is prompt space: the single-model prompt carries only the rules, ours carries the rules, the guidance and the cue words. Holding length constant, the acknowledgement difference disappears (p = 0.36) and the rule difference sits on the edge of significance (p = 0.04). The judges' scores are practically indistinguishable (8.68 against 8.78, p = 0.16).

At an equal generator, then, the decoupled design expresses the person's emotion more often, scores higher on an independent classifier, repeats itself less, writes shorter replies and allows the emotional decision to be logged or intercepted, paying for that with some rule adherence. It is an exchange, not a clean win.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:5.6}

This section reads the results as a whole: what they say about the research question, what the work cannot claim, and what should come next.

\subsection{Answer to the Research Question}
\label{sec:5.6.1}

The thesis asked whether separating emotion detection from response generation produces better empathetic replies than asking one model to do both, on a single consumer GPU. The answer is a qualified yes, and Table~\ref{tab:5.6} contains the whole of it.

Read down that table, three claims hold and one does not. \textbf{It is parameter-efficient}: 52\% of the larger model's size, and second of eight on the empathy panel. \textbf{It is resource-efficient:} 3.40 GB of GPU memory against 5.47 GB, at a response time that is not reliably different. \textbf{It is more emotionally faithful than either:} 36.7\% of its replies express the person's true feeling, against 32.3\% and 27.0\%, and it repeats itself less than the same generator alone while writing 22\% fewer words. \textbf{What it does not win is rule adherence:} 60.0\% against 73.7\% and 82.3\%. Two cells are blank because the single Phi-4-mini baseline was never put through the resource measurement.

The gains come from the structure and not from the size of the generator, which is what the middle column establishes: the same generator alone scores 4.75 and 27.0\%, gaining nothing over the 1.5-billion-parameter single model (Section 5.5.5). The larger single model remains ahead on rules and on the empathy classifier, though the judges separate neither its empathy nor its overall score from ours after correction, and the people we asked preferred ours. The case for decoupling is therefore not that separation beats scale, but that separation produces an emotional decision that is written down, at a size that fits the hardware most people have.

\subsection{Why the Two-Stage Design Works}
\label{sec:5.6.2}

The most surprising result in this chapter is that the stage-1 choice agrees with the dataset's label \textit{less} often than the detector's own top prediction (42.3\% against 50.3\%), while the system built on it has the best emotional fidelity of any comparable design. Three observations reconcile this. First, EmpatheticDialogues gives each conversation one fine-grained label chosen before the speaker wrote anything, and since almost all disagreements are between neighbouring emotions, a choice that differs from the label is often still a fair reading. Second, replies built on a wrong label are no worse than replies built on a right one (Section 5.4.3). Third, the improvement appears when per-emotion guidance is added: telling the generator \textit{how} to answer loneliness, rather than only \textit{that} the person is lonely, changes what the reply says.

Because the comparison removed the selection, the guidance and the cue words together, their individual shares cannot be separated, though the accuracy result suggests the guidance carries most of the weight. The effect is also narrower than the design's advantage over single models: the judges rate the two-stage and top-1 configurations almost identically, so it appears in the finer measures.

\subsection{Objections, and When a Single Model Is the Better Choice}
\label{sec:5.6.3}

Two objections can be made, and both were tested rather than argued. \textbf{That a second model is unnecessary: }a single model of the same size, and our own generator used the same way, both produced less empathetic and less emotionally faithful replies; the detector costs 125 million parameters, and a larger one changed nothing. \textbf{That retrieval would do the job more simply:} showing the generator real conversations with the same emotion \cite{lewis2020rag} cut rule adherence in both versions we built.

There is also a case against our own design: if the only goal is the best reply and the hardware can hold a 7-billion-parameter model, the empathy panel favours the larger single model, though the 40 people we asked did not. Decoupling is the better choice when the emotional reasoning has to be visible: to audit why a reply took a particular tone, to show a clinician which emotion was read, or to place a safeguard before any text is generated. The two are not exclusive, and combining the detector with a larger generator is the obvious next experiment.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:5.6.5}

Seven limitations bound what this work can claim.

\textbf{Data:} EmpatheticDialogues is English, crowd-sourced and single-labelled, so neither the detection accuracy nor the suitability of the replies can be assumed to carry over to Bangla speakers or to people in real distress.

\textbf{Safety:} the label set has no category for suicidal ideation, and the system has no crisis safeguard beyond disclaimers.

\textbf{Measures:} all automatic measures are proxies; the acknowledgement check is keyword-based, the classifier was trained in another domain and rewards length, the judge panel has only two members, and emotional fidelity depends on our own detector.

\textbf{Scale of evidence:} 300 conversations for generation, 64 for the judges, and a human study of 40 participants over 8 situations whose ratings agreed with each other only weakly.

\textbf{Single turns:} the quantitative results all concern first replies; the agent was checked on scenarios rather than measured.

\textbf{Repetition:} 58.7\% of replies still reuse stock phrases.

\textbf{Efficiency:} memory and time were measured on one GPU over 50 messages.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:5.6.6}

\textbf{Immediate Priorities}

\textbf{Incorporate a pre-generation crisis filter:} required before any public deployment, answering from a set of reviewed safe responses \cite{who2023suicide}.

\textbf{Perform a component-wise ablation:} separate the emotion selection, the per-emotion guidance and the attention cues, so the contribution of each is known rather than inferred.

\textbf{Use a larger generator:} combine the detector and per-emotion prompting with a larger generator.

\textbf{Guidance written with practitioners:} the three universal rules come from published sources, but the per-emotion guidance was written by the authors, who are not clinicians, and is the one component with no external grounding. It should be developed with practising counsellors and clinical psychologists, by interviewing them about how they respond to each kind of situation and asking them to rewrite the 32 sets of instructions; such interviews concern professional practice, not any patient's records. The change is cheap, since the guidance is read at generation time and replacing it retrains nothing.

\textbf{Beyond English (Language Extension)}

\textbf{Compile localized data:} obtain or adapt emotion-labelled Bangla conversations, including the code-mixed Bangla-English people here actually type.

\textbf{Retrain the detector:} from a multilingual encoder, which is cheap at 125 million parameters.

\textbf{Rewrite per-emotion guidance:} develop it with native speakers rather than translating from English, since how loneliness or shame should be answered is not the same everywhere. The decoupled structure helps: a new language needs a new detector and new guidance, not a new generator.

\textbf{Subsequent Steps}

\textbf{Control repetition:} constrain it at decoding time.

\textbf{Evaluate on real data:} measure whole multi-turn conversations and collect ratings from mental-health professionals.

\textbf{Measure hardware performance:} benchmark memory and response time on other hardware, including machines without a graphics card.
