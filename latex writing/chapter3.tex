\chapter{Requirements, Impacts and Constraints}
\label{ch:3}

A system that answers people describing painful experiences is judged on more than accuracy. It should run on the hardware a student team owns, respect the licence of the data it learns from, never misuse what a person types to it, and never pretend to offer help it is not qualified to give. This chapter sets out the requirements written against those demands, the system's impact on people and the environment, the ethical considerations it carries, the standards followed, and how the project, its risks and its costs were managed.

\section{Final Specifications and Requirements}
\label{sec:3.1}

Technically, two models had to run together on one consumer graphics card and answer within a few seconds: a RoBERTa-base classifier returning the three most likely emotions with the words behind the decision, and a 3.8-billion-parameter instruction model, quantised to 4 bits, that picks one and writes the reply. Methodologically, every design decision had to be settled by measurement on the same held-out conversations, and every number reproducible from saved outputs. The requirements below were fixed before the final round of development and can each be checked.

\subsection{Functional Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Emotion detection from the users' actual words:} the emotion is read from the message the user actually writes, over the 32 categories of EmpatheticDialogues \cite{rashkin2019}.
  \item \textbf{Candidate emotions, not a single guess:} the detector returns several probable emotions, each with a confidence percentage.
  \item \textbf{Visible emotional decision:} each response carries all the candidates, the emotion chosen and the words behind it.
  \item \textbf{Guided generation:} the generator is given wording written for the emotion chosen.
  \item \textbf{Response rules: }each response validates the emotion, poses a single question and stays within 120 words.
  \item \textbf{Multi-turn conversation: }the parts sustain a conversation over several turns and register when the emotion shifts.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Non-Functional Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hardware budget:} the whole system runs on one consumer GPU with 8 GB of memory \cite{nvidia_rtx4060}.
  \item \textbf{Privacy by locality:} no user message has to leave the device.
  \item \textbf{Response time: }a few seconds on that hardware.
  \item \textbf{Reproducibility:} numbers can be reproduced from saved outputs using fixed seeds.
  \item \textbf{Inspectability: }each component can be replaced and tested on its own.
  \item \textbf{Cost:} only open models, datasets and free tiers of services are used.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Hardware and Software Requirements}

\textbf{Hardware specifications:} all training, generation and benchmarking ran on one machine with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 GPU with 8 GB of video memory, 16 GB of system memory and a solid-state disk, under Windows 11. The card was not chosen as a restriction for its own sake: the goal was a system a counselling service can run on a typical computer. It sets the ceiling the design works under, since the detector, the quantised generator and their activations must all fit into 8 GB, and Chapter 5's 3.40 GB peak memory and 4.17 s per reply were measured on it.

\textbf{Software requirements:} Python 3.10 with PyTorch 2.5.1 and CUDA 12.1 \cite{paszke2019pytorch} and Hugging Face Transformers \cite{wolf2020transformers} for the encoder and generator, PEFT for low-rank adaptation and bitsandbytes for 4-bit NF4 quantisation, without which the generator would not fit in 8 GB. SciPy \cite{virtanen2020scipy} and scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011sklearn} provided the paired tests and classification metrics, sentence-transformers \cite{reimers2019sbert} the retrieval baseline, and the WASSA 2024 empathy classifier \cite{giorgi2024wassa} with the OpenRouter and Groq APIs the judges. The demonstration uses Gradio and the human study Google Forms; library versions are in the appendix.

\subsection{Data Requirements}

The data had to be conversational with fine-grained emotions. EmpatheticDialogues provides 23,144 conversations and 88,687 utterances, each carrying one of 32 emotion labels, split into 17,839 conversations for training, 2,763 for validation and 2,542 for testing \cite{rashkin2019}. The detector is trained on the first message of each, the text it receives in use, and reply quality is evaluated on 300 held-out conversations, 64 of them stratified for the judges. The corpus is released under a non-commercial research licence \cite{ed_github}.

\subsection{Design Constraints}

Several constraints shaped the design rather than merely restricting it. The 8 GB budget rules out an unquantised generator and any model above 8 billion parameters, which is why the generator is quantised and the detector small. The dataset gives each conversation one label although a conversation may carry several, so accuracy against the reference can never be perfect. Free evaluation services cap requests per day, so the judges scored only 64 conversations per system, and volunteer availability limited the human study to a short questionnaire. One GPU shared by three members against a fixed deadline meant experiments were ordered by cost: cheap local runs first, rate-limited ones only for designs already shown to be worth it.

\section{Societal Impact}
\label{sec:3.2}

\subsection{Potential Benefits}

The system is built for the moment when somebody needs to be heard and no trained listener is available. It offers six benefits.

\textbf{Privacy for people who won't talk to a cloud service:} both models run on the user's own machine, so a disclosure about grief, loneliness or fear never leaves it, which for many is the difference between writing the message and not writing it at all.

\textbf{A listener available at every hour: }distress keeps no office hours, and a locally hosted system answers at three in the morning or after the counselling centre has closed.

\textbf{A first step towards professional help:} it is not a therapist and must not replace one, but it can start the first conversation and make reaching a counsellor feel less daunting.

\textbf{Low cost to the institutions that need it most: }it runs on one computer at a counselling service, small clinic or helpline with no per-message cost. In Bangladesh, where professionals are few and private counselling is beyond most students, the cost of access matters as much as the reply.

\textbf{A way to avoid stigma: }many people will type a worry they would never say aloud to a relative or a doctor.

\textbf{Replies that can be checked before they are seen: }the emotion behind each turn is written down, so a supervisor can check it and a safeguard can hold the reply back, where a single end-to-end model offers nothing to inspect.

\subsection{Risks and Limits of Use}

Three risks are stated plainly, because a system that sounds caring may be trusted further than it deserves.

\textbf{Use as a substitute: }someone in distress may keep talking to the machine instead of reaching a person who can help. With no crisis filter and no category for suicidal thoughts, it must never be deployed as a mental-health service; any use needs a reviewed filter, a referral path and a visible helpline number \cite{who2023suicide}.

\textbf{Wrong or shallow empathy: }the detector's first guess is correct 50.3\% of the time and the reply names the user's actual emotion 36.7\% of the time, and a reply built on the wrong emotion can read as dismissive. Passing three candidates reduces that cost, but for one person at one moment the risk is real.

\textbf{Cultural and linguistic fit:} EmpatheticDialogues was written by English-speaking crowdworkers \cite{rashkin2019}, and feelings are expressed differently across languages, so data and evaluation in Bangla would be needed before use in Bangladesh.

In sum, the design gives an institution privacy, affordability and an auditable emotional decision, paid for with replies the judges do not rate above those of one larger model.

\section{Environmental Impact}
\label{sec:3.3}

The environmental cost of this work is the electricity used to train and test the models, which is small because the models are small. Everything ran on a single RTX 4060 rated at 115 W \cite{nvidia_rtx4060}. Taking rated power times GPU hours, which overestimates the real draw, the final detector used about 0.017 kWh and the RoBERTa-large alternative about 0.124 kWh. The heaviest loads were the fourteen configurations of 300 or more conversations each and the fine-tuning runs, a few kilowatt hours in all, about what a desktop draws over a few days. No cloud GPU was rented and nothing trained from scratch. The design also lowers the cost of running the system: within statistical error it matches a single model with 1.9 times its parameters on 38\% less GPU memory, so a service that would otherwise need a data-centre GPU can run on a mid-range consumer card, and no message travels to a data centre and back. No new hardware was bought, and the 8 GB limit was set so none would be needed.

\section{Ethical Issues}
\label{sec:3.4}

Answering someone who is upset carries responsibilities no accuracy score captures. Seven shaped this project.

\textbf{Safety and scope}

The system is meant for supportive listening in low-risk conversations and is neither a therapeutic, a diagnostic nor a crisis service. The most dangerous case is a user saying they are thinking of killing themselves: there is no category for suicidal ideation among the 32 of EmpatheticDialogues \cite{rashkin2019}, so the detector cannot represent such a message. Tested with ``I want to die'', it read \textit{afraid }(45\%) and \textit{terrified }(28\%), and the reply was warm but named no crisis service, where commercial assistants answered mostly with helpline information. That is not sufficient, since World Health Organization guidance calls for understanding together with accurate information on where to get help \cite{who2023suicide}. Mitigation here is limited to the interface, which states the system is an unmonitored student project and lists crisis services. A pre-generation crisis filter is essential before deployment, and the decoupled design makes it possible to add.

\textbf{Clinical responsibility}

The system produces text that reads like emotional support, but nobody clinically qualified is answering. Three commitments follow: it will never be presented as a counsellor, therapist or helpline; the decision behind each reply is recorded and auditable; and before deployment it must be reviewed by a qualified professional, run with a crisis filter and a referral path, and have a named person accountable for it.

\textbf{Protection of personal data and privacy}

All models were built and tested on the publicly available EmpatheticDialogues dataset. No personal data was collected; only dataset text and the replies generated from it reached the judge services. Both models run locally, so a deployed instance sends no disclosure to an external provider, and the demonstration holds conversation history in memory only.

\textbf{Human evaluation}

The study in Chapter 5 had volunteers, mostly students with a few faculty members, rate replies to 8 short situations. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, no names or addresses were collected, and participants were told how long it would take (15 minutes), warned that some scenarios involve distressing experiences, and free to stop at any point. Reply order was counterbalanced, so nobody could be steered towards the authors' system.

\textbf{Data and model licensing}

EmpatheticDialogues is published under CC BY-NC 4.0 \cite{ed_github}, so a detector trained on it must be attributed and cannot go into a commercial product. Phi-4-mini-instruct is MIT-licensed and the RoBERTa baselines Apache-2.0, while the Gemma-3 candidate carries Google's Gemma Terms of Use \cite{gemma_terms}. The WASSA empathy model has no licence and is used for evaluation only, with credit to its authors.

\textbf{Bias and fairness}

EmpatheticDialogues is an English-only crowdworker corpus, so neither detection accuracy nor the suitability of the replies is guaranteed to transfer to users in Bangladesh. The evaluation carries its own bias: the acknowledgement check looks for phrases such as ``I'm sorry'', which suit negative emotions and under-credit good replies to positive ones, so it is never used alone.

\textbf{Research integrity}

Negative findings are reported in full: retrieval augmentation, the fine-tuned generator, the Gemma-3 candidate, the larger detector, and the finding that our advantage over a same-size single model in rule adherence did not survive correction. A configuration error found in the fine-tuned baseline was retrained and every affected number replaced rather than hidden, and every figure is rebuilt from saved results by scripts in the repository.

\section{Standards}
\label{sec:3.5}

There are no engineering standards for an empathetic dialogue system, so the project followed published practice for its components. All data and models are used under their licences, EmpatheticDialogues only for non-commercial research. The response rules follow guidance for supportive conversation: the emotional-reaction mechanism of EPITOME \cite{sharma2020epitome} for the acknowledgement rule, and counselling guidance against the question-and-answer trap \cite{samhsa2019tip35} for the single-question rule. The classifier comes from the WASSA 2024 shared task \cite{giorgi2024wassa}, and the judge evaluation follows accepted practice: blind, anchored to a written rubric, at temperature 0, with judges from unrelated developers \cite{verga2024}. Statistical claims use paired tests with correction for multiple comparisons (Section 5.4.1). The one requirement the work fails is the World Health Organization's: it has no crisis safeguard and must not be used as a mental-health service \cite{who2023suicide}. It also follows BRAC University's academic conduct requirements: sources cited, the team's own results reported, errors recorded rather than removed.

\section{Project Management Plan}
\label{sec:3.6}

The project ran for 9 months with a team of three, one shared GPU machine and a fixed deadline. The work was not linear but a series of overlapping phases of experimentation, evaluation and writing, so a result that challenged an assumption could be absorbed without halting the rest. Development was evidence-gated: every design alternative ran on the same 300 held-out conversations, scored on the same measures, and was kept only if the numbers supported it, with cheap local measures as the first gate and the rate-limited judges and human study spent only on candidates that passed it. Figure~\ref{fig:3.1} shows the five phases.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Phase 1 - Background study and candidate selection (January to May 2026):} the problem was defined, the literature reviewed, and first detectors and generators trained on GoEmotions.
  \item \textbf{Phase 2 - Move to EmpatheticDialogues (June 2026): }the comments proved unsuitable for a conversational system, so the work restarted on a conversational corpus, giving the first detector on the new data.
  \item \textbf{Phase 3 - Evaluation of the initial pipeline (July to August 2026): }the first complete pipeline was measured against zero-shot, fine-tuned and single-model baselines with an AI-judge panel, an empathy classifier and paired tests.
  \item \textbf{Phase 4 - Design adjustment (August to September 2026):} that evidence drove the corrected detector, the generator comparison, the two-stage emotion selection, per-emotion guidance and attention cues, giving the final system.
  \item \textbf{Phase 5 - Validation, agent and writing (September 2026):} robustness checks, multi-turn agent, human evaluation and this report.
\end{itemize}

\section{Risk Management}
\label{sec:3.7}

A risk was recorded whenever an experiment behaved oddly or a phase had to be revisited, and rated for probability and for its effect on results, schedule or user safety. Mitigation was structural wherever possible, an automatic check, a fixed seed or a pinned version, so the same failure could not recur undetected. Of twelve risks, eight occurred, two were avoided and two remain open; three changed how the project works.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Silent technical failure:} the design asks the detector for the attention weights behind its prediction, but the library's default implementation returns nothing and raises no error, so cue extraction fell back to text splitting while appearing to work. Undiscovered, the thesis would have described a mechanism that was not running. An automatic check now halts execution when the weights are missing.
  \item \textbf{Validity of the results:} the original detector was trained on situation descriptions and used on the user's own messages, scoring 56.9\% against 48.2\% on the real input, so it was retrained. A RoBERTa-large run with a missing warm-up collapsed to 5.0\% accuracy; the cause was found and the run repeated at 53.0\%.
  \item \textbf{Dependence on external services:} the judges are third-party services outside the team's control, and several candidates were retired or restricted during the project. Every system is scored by the two that remained available throughout, and rate-limited work was split across two accounts with every emotion represented in both halves.
\end{itemize}
Two risks remain open. Judge scores and human-evaluation items would have to be discarded if the design changed at the last minute, and the gain from a repetition penalty is left for later. Beyond interface disclaimers the system still has no safeguard for a disclosure of suicidal thinking (Section 3.4).

\section{Economic Analysis}
\label{sec:3.8}

The project was built to be as close to cost-free as possible, since a student team faces the constraint a small clinic does. Every model is openly released, every library open source, the dataset free for research, the judges free-tier and the human study run on a free questionnaire service, so direct spending is limited to electricity, internet access and a GPU workstation the team already owned. Training the final detector took 8.8 minutes and RoBERTa-large 64.7, so at the energy figures of Section 3.3 the cost of training is negligible.

\subsection{Cost-Benefit of the Main Design Choices}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Where the cheaper choice won: }RoBERTa-large is 2.9 points more accurate than RoBERTa-base but 2.8 times larger and 7.4 times slower to train, and changed no measure of reply quality in the final system. Fine-tuning the generator likewise cost 43 minutes of GPU time to reach 22.3\% rule adherence, where the instruction-tuned model needed no training and reached 60.0\%.
  \item \textbf{Where it did not:} a single Qwen2.5-7B model beats our pipeline on rule adherence, empathy-classifier score and judge empathy, so for quality alone it is the better choice; but on the same GPU our system uses 38\% less peak memory while reaching 97.5\% of that model's overall judge score.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation method:} the human study confirms the free-tier judges rather than being replaced by them. Human raters are paid roughly USD 6 per hour in one published study, while the judges scored several hundred replies at no cost.
\end{itemize}
