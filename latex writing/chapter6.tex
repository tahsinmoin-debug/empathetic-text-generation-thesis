\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:6}

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:6.1}

The goal was to build an empathetic response system with emotion detection separated from response generation, within the 8 GB of an ordinary consumer graphics card, and to measure whether the separation is worthwhile. From 4,200 scored replies across fourteen configurations the answer is a qualified yes.

The first part built the detector: five encoders and decoders trained the same way, from which RoBERTa-base was chosen at 50.3\% top-1 and 84.3\% top-5 accuracy over the 32 emotions of EmpatheticDialogues, outperforming every jointly trained model published on this corpus. Two findings matter more than that number: the detector's mistakes are almost always near neighbours, afraid read as terrified and sad as devastated; and what it is trained on matters more than which network is chosen, since training on situation descriptions while using it on the user's own message cost around nine points. Both led to passing three candidates instead of one.

The second part built and tested the pipeline, measuring five ways of connecting the two models with the prompt fixed and three generators with the detector and prompt fixed. The chosen design, in which Phi-4-mini selects one of the three candidates and then writes under per-emotion guidance, comes second of eight at +0.59 on the empathy panel against +1.11 for a single model nearly double its size. Two comparisons carry the argument: the same generator alone scores +0.53, so the gain comes from the design rather than the generator's size; and 36.7\% of replies express the speaker's true emotion, 11.0 points above a single model of similar size. It does this on 3.40 GB of peak memory, 3.96 billion parameters and 4.17 seconds per reply, 38\% less memory than a model with 1.9 times the parameters and no significant cost in response time. The human study settles what the automatic measures left open: 40 participants rated it highest on all three scales and preferred it in 61.3\% of choices against 21.6\%.

Four findings qualify the claim. The largest single model still leads the empathy panel, and anyone with the hardware to run a 7-billion-parameter model and no need for an auditable decision should use one. The three rules are met in 60.0\% of replies against 73.7\% for the same generator alone, because they share prompt space with the guidance. The judges do not distinguish the two configurations, so the case rests on the empathy classifier, emotional fidelity, lower repetition and the visible decision rather than judge preference. And a detector three points more accurate changed no measure of reply quality, so the detector was not the limiting component.

\section{Contributions to the Field}
\label{sec:6.2}

\textbf{First,} the work gives a calibrated account of what a decoupled design costs and buys at the scale where the choice matters. Pairing a small emotion model with a large one has been proposed before, but not tested under a hardware constraint against single models of both its own size and a larger one.

\textbf{Second,} it records negative outcomes that can save others time: a detector three points more accurate improved no measure of the reply, so its value lies in the structure it provides rather than the precision of its label, and retrieval augmentation cut rule adherence from 63.7\% to 43.7\% for no gain.

\textbf{Third,} it makes the emotional decision visible: every reply carries the three candidate emotions, the one selected and the words behind it. An end-to-end model can write the same reply but cannot show which feeling it answered, so a wrong reading can be neither intercepted nor reviewed.

\textbf{Fourth,} it offers a reusable evaluation design: explicit checks of what a supportive reply must contain, an empathy panel of two judges from different developers beside a classifier trained on human ratings, and paired testing with correction for multiple comparisons.

\textbf{Fifth,} it is not only a benchmark entry: the detector's context window, the generator's memory and rules that relax after the first turn make it an agent rather than an interrogation.

\textbf{Sixth,} it records the practical failures a reproduction would meet: a model that never learns to stop when the wrong padding token is used, and an attention implementation that returns no weights.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:6.3}

Seven limitations bound these conclusions, set out in full in Section 5.6.4. The corpus is English, crowd-sourced and single-labelled, so nothing here is representative of Bangla speakers or of people in real distress. The label set has no category for suicidal ideation and the system no crisis safeguard. Every automatic measure is a proxy, and emotional fidelity is assessed by our own detector. The evidence rests on 300 conversations, 64 of them judged, and 40 people whose ratings agreed only weakly. All quantitative results concern first replies, repetition remains high at 58.7\%, and the cost figures come from one graphics card over 50 messages.

\section{Recommendations for Future Work}
\label{sec:6.4}

\textbf{Immediate extensions:} a crisis filter reading the message before generation and answering from a set of reviewed safe responses is needed before this system is placed in front of anyone, and is the first work we recommend \cite{who2023suicide}. Next, a component-wise ablation separating the emotion choice, the guidance and the attention cues, so each contribution is known rather than inferred. The rule-adherence gap can be closed by repairing the reply after it is written, and repetition controlled at decoding time; both were deferred to keep the evaluated outputs fixed.

\textbf{Beyond English:} the decoupled structure helps, since a new language needs a new detector and new guidance but not a new generator, and the detector is only 125 million parameters. Three steps follow: collecting or adapting emotional conversations in Bangla, including the code-mixed Bangla-English people here actually type; retraining the detector from a multilingual encoder; and rewriting the guidance with speakers of the language, since a translated prompt would carry English assumptions into the reply. Work on low-resource emotion classification puts the difficulty with the detector \cite{belay2025}.

\textbf{Stronger evaluation:} whole conversations should be rated, not only single replies, and the human study repeated at a size that supports statistical testing, ideally with mental-health professionals as raters. The panel would benefit from more members, and the cost figures should be repeated on other hardware, including machines without a graphics card.

\textbf{Final remarks:} this work is not a new record on a benchmark and was not designed to be one. Its contribution is to show by measurement rather than argument that a system built from a small specialised model and a modest general one can be more emotionally responsive than a single model of its own size, can run on hardware a student already owns so a person's disclosure never leaves their machine, and can show exactly what it understood before it answers. As capable open models keep getting smaller, how they are arranged will matter more than how large they are, and a sensitive application will need a design whose emotional reasoning can be inspected, corrected and replaced one part at a time.
