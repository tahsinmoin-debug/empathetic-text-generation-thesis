This section interprets the results as a whole: what they mean for the research question, why the unexpected results occurred, how the work answers the concerns of the Pre-Thesis 2 panel, what its limitations are, and what should follow.

## 5.6.1 Answer to the Research Question

This thesis asked whether separating emotion detection from response generation produces better empathetic replies than asking a single model to perform both tasks, under the constraint of a single consumer GPU. The experiments give a qualified answer. **At comparable scale, the decoupled design produces replies that are significantly more empathetic according to both the WASSA classifier and the AI judges, and significantly more faithful to the user's actual emotion**, while its rule adherence is statistically indistinguishable from that of a same-size single model after correction for multiple comparisons. **A larger single model, however, outperforms it** in rule adherence and empathy, although the AI judges' overall scores for the two do not differ significantly after correction (8.90 against 8.68). The advantage of decoupling therefore does not lie in separation alone outperforming scale. It lies in what separation makes possible: an explicit and inspectable emotional decision that can be conditioned on, audited and, where necessary, intercepted.

## 5.6.2 Why the Two-Stage Design Works

The most unexpected result is that the final system's emotion selection agrees with the dataset's gold label *less* often than the detector's own top prediction (42.3% against 50.3%), yet the final system achieves the highest emotion validation and the strongest empathy scores among comparable designs. Three observations reconcile these findings. First, EmpatheticDialogues assigns each conversation a single fine-grained label chosen by the speaker before the conversation [1], while many messages plausibly express a neighbouring feeling. Most detector errors are exactly such neighbours, such as *afraid* and *terrified* or *sentimental* and *nostalgic*, so a choice that disagrees with the gold label is often still a reasonable reading of the text. Second, the error-propagation analysis shows that replies based on an incorrect label are no worse in rule adherence or empathy: what a reply needs is an emotion close enough to respond to, not the exact gold label. Third, the improvement appears when emotion-specific guidance is added. Telling the generator *how* to respond to loneliness, rather than only *that* the user is lonely, changes the substance of the reply. Because our comparison removed the emotion selection, the guidance and the attention cues together, their contributions cannot be separated. However, the accuracy result suggests that the guidance, rather than the selection, accounts for most of the improvement. This improvement is also narrower than the design's advantage over single models: the AI judges rate the two-stage and top-1 Phi-4-mini configurations almost identically (overall 8.68 against 8.65, p = 0.71), so the benefit of the two-stage design appears in the finer measures of empathy and emotion fidelity rather than in overall judge ratings.

## 5.6.3 Response to the Pre-Thesis 2 Panel

The panel questioned whether multiple models were necessary and suggested that a simpler agentic, retrieval-based design might perform better. Both suggestions were tested directly. A single model of the same size, prompted to identify the emotion and respond in one step, produced less empathetic and less emotionally faithful replies than the decoupled design. A larger single model performed better in raw quality, which we report as a genuine trade-off (Section 5.6.4). Retrieving similar human conversations [11] reduced rule adherence in both versions we built, from 63.7% to 54.3% and 43.7%, because real listener replies model the multi-question behaviour that the rules are intended to prevent. We did not build the full multi-turn agentic system the panel described, so our evidence concerns retrieval-augmented prompting rather than agentic systems in general. The second model justifies its place as a component for transparency and control: it exposes the emotional decision at a cost of 125 million parameters and under ten minutes of training. A larger detector would not change this conclusion, since substituting RoBERTa-large into the final system left reply quality unchanged.

## 5.6.4 When a Larger Single Model Is the Better Choice

If the only goal is the highest-quality reply on hardware able to run a 7-billion-parameter model, our results favour the larger single model, which led in rule adherence (82.3%), WASSA empathy (5.76) and AI-judge empathy (8.15 against 7.84). The decoupled design is preferable when the system's emotional reasoning must be visible, for example to audit why a reply adopted a particular tone, to show users or clinicians which emotion was detected, or to insert a safeguard, such as a crisis filter, before any text is generated. It is also preferable when components must be replaceable independently. The two approaches are not mutually exclusive: the detector and emotion-specific prompting could be combined with a larger generator, which we leave to future work.

## 5.6.5 Limitations

This work has seven main limitations. (1) **Data.** EmpatheticDialogues is an English, crowd-sourced corpus with a single label per conversation [1], so neither detection accuracy nor the appropriateness of replies can be assumed to transfer to Bangla speakers or to people in genuine distress. (2) **Safety.** The label set contains no category for suicidal ideation, and the system has no crisis safeguard beyond interface disclaimers (Section 3.4). (3) **Evaluation.** The automatic measures are proxies. The acknowledgement check is keyword-based and biased toward negative emotions. The WASSA classifier was trained on peer discussions of news articles, produces coarse classes and rewards length [14]. Emotion validation relies on our own detector. AI judges may favour longer answers [13], and the planned three-judge panel was reduced to two judges when one became unavailable, although the two panels agree closely on the systems both scored. (4) **Scale of evidence.** Generation results are based on 300 conversations and AI-judge results on 64, and the human study uses a small set of items rated by non-expert participants [[PENDING: final number of participants]]. (5) **Single-turn evaluation.** The quantitative results concern single turns; the multi-turn agent was assessed only on scenario tests. (6) **Repetition.** The final system reuses stock phrases in 58.7% of its replies, consistent with the repetitiveness reported for LLM empathy [29]. (7) **Efficiency.** Inference latency and memory use were not measured.

## 5.6.6 Future Work

In order of priority, future work should: (i) add a pre-generation crisis filter with reviewed safe responses, which is required before any public deployment [36]; (ii) conduct a component-wise ablation that separates the effects of emotion selection, emotion-specific guidance and attention cues; (iii) combine the detector and emotion-specific prompting with a larger generator; (iv) introduce decoding-level control of repetition, such as a repetition penalty of 1.1; (v) evaluate the system on real multi-turn conversations and with mental-health professionals; (vi) develop a Bangla or code-mixed dataset for the detector; and (vii) measure inference latency and memory use to inform deployment decisions.
