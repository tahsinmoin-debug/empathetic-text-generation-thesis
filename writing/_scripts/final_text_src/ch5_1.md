# 5.1 Performance Evaluation

This section reports the performance of the final system on every evaluation signal, with all alternative designs shown for comparison. The reasons for the differences between designs are analysed in Section 5.2, and their statistical significance in Section 5.3.

## 5.1.1 Experimental Setup

All generation systems respond to the same 300 conversations, sampled with seed 42 from the EmpatheticDialogues test partition {cite:rashkin2019}, using identical decoding settings (Section 4.1.4). The detector is evaluated on all 2,542 test conversations. The systems are referred to by the names given in Table 5.1, and the **final system** is the two-stage pipeline with a RoBERTa-base detector and a Phi-4-mini generator.

## 5.1.2 Emotion Detection

Table 5.2 reports the performance of the final detector. RoBERTa-base reaches **50.3% top-1**, **76.5% top-3** and **84.3% top-5** accuracy, with a macro-F1 score of 49.4%, against a chance level of 3.1% for 32 classes. Performance varies considerably across emotions (Table 5.3). *Caring* (F1 70.1%), *lonely* (69.5%) and *jealous* (64.7%) are recognised most reliably, while *angry* (18.2%), *ashamed* (22.2%) and *joyful* (30.9%) are the most difficult. Table 5.4 shows that the most frequent errors occur between closely related labels: *sentimental* is read as *nostalgic* (31 cases), *afraid* as *terrified* (29), *angry* as *furious* (24) and *sad* as *devastated* (23). Many errors are therefore differences in intensity or nuance within the same family of emotions rather than confusions between unrelated feelings, consistent with the error pattern reported for a comparable classifier {cite:paranjape2020chirpy}. This is why the final design passes the three most probable emotions to the generator rather than a single label.

## 5.1.3 Rule Adherence

Table 5.1 and Figure 5.1 show how often each system's replies acknowledge the user's feeling, ask exactly one question and stay within 120 words. The final system follows all three rules in **60.0%** of replies: 66.3% contain an acknowledgement, 92.7% ask exactly one question and 100% stay within the length limit, with an average length of 57.1 words. Among systems of comparable size, it is exceeded by the top-1 Phi-4-mini configuration (65.3%) and the initial pipeline (63.7%), and it is well ahead of the same-size single model (51.3%), zero-shot prompting (19.0%) and the monolithic fine-tuned model (0.0%). The larger single model, Qwen2.5-7B, has the highest rule adherence at 82.3%. The monolithic fine-tuned model shows the failure the rules were designed to prevent: it averages 7.07 questions and 200.7 words per reply, and 98.0% of its replies ask more than one question.

## 5.1.4 Empathy Classifier Score

Figure 5.2 shows the WASSA empathy scores {cite:yang2024hyy33} with 95% confidence intervals. The final system scores **5.05**, the highest of all systems no larger than Phi-4-mini, and above the same-size single model (4.84), the initial pipeline (4.80), zero-shot prompting (4.25) and the fine-tuned model (4.19). Gemma-3-4B (5.30) and the larger single model (5.76) score higher, and Section 5.4 shows that Gemma's advantage is explained by the length of its replies. The classifier outputs coarse ordinal classes, and our replies fall between classes 1 and 7. It was also trained on empathy ratings of turns in peer discussions of news articles {cite:giorgi2024wassa}. We therefore interpret differences between system averages rather than absolute values; Section 5.3.6 shows that it ranks systems in exactly the same order as the AI judges.

[[FIGURE 5.2 | fig_wassa_empathy_ci.png | WASSA empathy score of each system with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals]]

## 5.1.5 Emotion Fidelity

To check whether a reply expresses the *right* emotion, and not merely a well-formed one, each reply is passed back through the final detector. We compare the detector's reading of the reply with the true label (**emotion validation**) and with the emotion the system conditioned on (**context awareness**). The final system has the highest emotion validation of all systems at **36.7%**, compared with 32.3% for the larger single model, 28.3% for the top-1 Phi-4-mini configuration, 26.0% for the initial pipeline and 25.7% for the same-size single model. Its context awareness is 54.3%. Two limitations apply to these measures. The detector was trained on user messages rather than replies, so its reading of a reply is noisy. And context awareness uses the same detector that supplied the signal to our pipelines, which may favour them. Emotion validation, which compares against the dataset's independent gold label, is therefore the more trustworthy of the two.

## 5.1.6 Behaviour of the Two-Stage Emotion Selection

Table 5.5 examines the stage-1 decision. The true emotion is among the detector's three best candidates in 78.0% of the 300 evaluation conversations, and every stage-1 output names a valid candidate. The generator overrides the detector's top choice in 40.7% of cases, and its choice matches the gold label in **42.3%** of conversations. This is *lower* than the detector's own top-1 accuracy on the same conversations (50.3%). The final system's gains in empathy and emotion validation therefore do not come from selecting the gold label more often, a finding discussed in Section 5.6.

## 5.1.7 Diversity and Repetition

Table 5.6 reports distinct-n diversity together with a boilerplate rate, defined as the proportion of replies that contain one of the system's ten most frequent five-word phrases. The final system's Dist-2 of 47.8% is close to that of the initial pipeline (49.5%), but its boilerplate rate is higher (58.7% against 23.7%). Its most repeated phrase, "how are you feeling right", appears in 49 replies. Phi-4-mini prompted with a single generic prompt is the most repetitive system (77.0%), so emotion-specific prompts reduce repetition for the same generator but do not eliminate it, consistent with the repetitive empathic phrasing reported for LLMs {cite:sorin2024jmir}. Because these values are computed over 300 replies, they are not comparable with distinct-n scores published for the full test set.

## 5.1.8 AI-Judge Evaluation

Three AI judges, Nemotron 3 Ultra, Mistral Large and gpt-oss-120b, selected as described in Section 4.1.5.1, score each reply blind from 1 to 10 for empathy, relevance and safety. They are applied to a stratified sample of 64 conversations, consisting of the shortest and longest message for each of the 32 emotions. Table 5.7 reports the results. Among the systems judged so far, the larger single model has the highest average score (9.02), followed by the same-size single model (8.38), the initial pipeline (8.23), zero-shot prompting (7.73) and the fine-tuned model (4.44). ## 5.1.9 Human Evaluation

To validate the automatic and AI-judge results, we conducted a forced-choice study with human participants (Table 5.8). Participants read 13 situations and, for each one, chose which of three replies "best understands and responds to how this person is feeling": the reply from the final system, from the same-size single model, or from the larger single model, presented in counterbalanced order. This three-way design adapts the pairwise preference judgements used in comparable studies {cite:cao2025tooled, wu2025traits} to compare three systems within a limited number of items. The situations cover 13 different emotions, including four central to supportive conversation (sad, lonely, angry and anxious) and three drawn from student life. Two items include an obviously poor reply as an attention check.
