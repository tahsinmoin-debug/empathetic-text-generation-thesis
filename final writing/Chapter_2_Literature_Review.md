# Chapter 2: Literature Review

## 2.1 Preliminaries

**Empathy as something that can be measured:** In everyday use empathy is a vague word, and a system cannot be built against a vague word. The framework this work relies on is EPITOME, developed for text-based mental-health support and grounded in established empathy scales, which separates a reply into three communication mechanisms: *emotional reactions*, where the reply names and validates what the person feels; *interpretations*, where it shows it has understood the situation; and *explorations*, where it invites the person to say more [2]. Counselling practice adds a constraint from the other direction: guidance for motivational interviewing warns that stacking questions produces a "question-and-answer trap" in which the person becomes a passive respondent [3]. Taken together these give a working definition of a good supportive reply that a machine can be held to: acknowledge the feeling, respond to the specific situation, and invite without interrogating.

**The corpus:** EmpatheticDialogues is the standard benchmark for this task [4]. One crowdworker was given one of 32 emotion labels, wrote a situation description, and then described that situation in conversation; a second worker replied as a listener without being told the label. The released files hold 24,850 conversations, divided by the authors into training, validation and test partitions with no speaker appearing in more than one [5]. Two properties of the corpus matter throughout this thesis: each conversation carries exactly one emotion label, chosen before the conversation was written; and the *situation description* and the *first message* are different texts, the former written about the event and the latter written to a listener.

**GoEmotions** is the other corpus commonly used for fine-grained emotion, containing about 58,000 Reddit comments labelled with 27 emotions plus neutral, and allowing more than one label per comment [31]. This project began on GoEmotions and moved to EmpatheticDialogues; Section 4.3.1 gives the four measured reasons for that change.

**Recognising emotion in conversation:** Emotion recognition in conversation is a field in its own right, distinguished from sentence-level classification by the fact that the same words carry different feelings depending on what came before [18]. Related work maps the *intents* a listener's reply can express, such as questioning, acknowledging, consoling or encouraging, into a taxonomy derived from the same corpus [32], and shows that emotions in dialogue are frequently implicit, requiring inference rather than recognition [19], and frequently multiple, with a single utterance carrying more than one label [20].

**The models:** Every model in this work descends from the transformer [33]. Two families are used for two different jobs. **Encoders** read a whole text at once and are trained with masked-language modelling, which makes them suited to classification: BERT established the pattern [34] and RoBERTa improved it by training longer on far more text with dynamic masking [35]. **Decoder-only language models** generate text left to right and, when instruction-tuned, follow written rules; this work uses Qwen2.5 [36] and Phi-4-mini [37], the latter trained with a particular emphasis on instruction following.

**Making large models fit:** Two techniques make a multi-billion-parameter generator usable on a consumer GPU. **Quantisation** stores weights at reduced precision; 4-bit NF4 with double quantisation retains the quality of 16-bit weights at roughly a quarter of the memory [38]. **Low-rank adaptation** trains a small number of additional parameters instead of the whole network, making fine-tuning feasible on the same hardware [39]. A third technique, **retrieval-augmented generation**, supplies a model with relevant passages retrieved at inference time [40], typically found with sentence embeddings [41]; this work builds it as a baseline rather than as part of the final design.

**Measuring empathy:** Because no single automatic measure captures empathy, three kinds of evaluation are used here. The **WASSA shared task on empathy detection** provides a model trained on human empathy ratings of conversation turns [26], of which the hyy33 system is used in this work [27]. **Large language models as judges** correlate well with human preference when the judge is strong [28], but they favour their own generations [30], which is why a panel drawn from different developers is recommended over a single judge [29]. **Human evaluation** remains the reference, and recent empathetic-generation work reports it as either five-point ratings [13] or head-to-head preference [15].

## 2.2 Review of Existing Research

The study that created this field, published at ACL by Rashkin et al. (2019), introduced EmpatheticDialogues and with it the framing every later paper has used: a listener replies to someone describing an emotional situation, and the emotion is one of 32 fine-grained categories rather than a positive or negative sentiment. The paper also established the two ways of using the label, either supplying it to the generator or asking the model to predict it, and reported a fastText classifier at 43% accuracy on the situation description as a reference point for the recognition task [4].

A line of **joint models** followed, each adding a mechanism to a single encoder-decoder trained end to end on the corpus. MoEL (Lin et al., 2019) replaced the single decoder with a mixture of listeners, one per emotion, and combined their outputs with a softmax over the detected emotion [6]. MIME (Majumder et al., 2020) argued that a good listener partly mirrors the speaker's feeling and partly balances it, and built emotion mimicry and polarity grouping into the model [7]. EmpDG (Li et al., 2020) added an adversarial discriminator and multi-resolution emotion signals [8]. KEMP (Li et al., 2022) brought in external commonsense and concept knowledge so that the model could reason about what a situation implies rather than only what it says [9], and CEM (Sabour et al., 2022) extended that idea with commonsense inference about the speaker's reactions, intentions and needs [10]. CASE (Zhou et al., 2023) aligned a commonsense graph with a concept graph to connect the speaker's cognition and affection [11], and ESCM (Yang et al., 2023) used dynamic emotion-semantic vectors and emotional supervision [12]. Read as a sequence, these papers improve steadily on emotion accuracy, from 30.6% for EmpDG and 30.8% for MoEL through 36.6% for KEMP and 37.8% for CEM to 38.9% for CASE and 41.2% for ESCM, and the improvement comes from adding knowledge rather than from changing how the emotion is used. A related model, RoBERTa-GPT2 (Liu et al., 2021), is the exception that reveals the cause: by initialising the encoder from pre-trained RoBERTa and the decoder from pre-trained GPT-2 rather than training both from scratch, it reaches 51.5% on the same task [42], which is roughly ten points above the best knowledge-augmented joint model.

Work on the **listener's side of the conversation** approached the problem differently. Welivita and Pu (2020) annotated a large sample of EmpatheticDialogues and derived a taxonomy of empathetic response intents, showing that human listeners in this corpus rely on a small, describable set of behaviours such as questioning, acknowledging, consoling and encouraging [32]. Their finding matters for any rule-based approach, including ours, because it establishes that supportive replies have recognisable structure rather than being unconstrained text.

The arrival of **large language models** changed the comparison entirely. Qian et al. (2023) put ChatGPT against the purpose-built models above in a human evaluation and found that raters preferred it decisively, scoring it 4.64 out of 5 against 3.08 for CEM, and further showed that its replies improve when it is given the emotion explicitly or asked to reason about the speaker's situation before answering [13]. Loh and Raamkumar (2023) evaluated large language models directly against counselling criteria for online mental-health support and reached a similar conclusion about their capability while noting that their replies remain generic without structure [14]. Cao et al. (2025) proposed TOOL-ED, which treats a commonsense knowledge base as a tool the model may call during generation, so that the knowledge that KEMP and CEM built into the architecture is instead retrieved on demand [15]. Wu et al. (2025) conditioned generation on inferred personality traits, arguing that the same situation warrants different responses to different people, and reported 45.4% emotion accuracy for their personality-aware model [16]. Wang et al. (2025) applied a large language model to emotional support conversation, where the task extends from a single reply to a strategy across a dialogue [43], and Rasool et al. (2025) fused emotion-aware embeddings across several models for intelligent response generation [44]. Li et al. (2024) took the reasoning route, proposing an emotional chain-of-thought in which the model is prompted to work through the emotional situation in steps before producing its answer [17].

Whether these models actually *recognise* emotion well is a separate question, and the evidence is mixed. Feng et al. (2023) evaluated large language models on affect recognition in conversation and found their performance competitive but not clearly superior to purpose-trained classifiers [21]. Belay et al. (2025) evaluated them on multi-label emotion understanding and found that identifying several co-occurring emotions remains difficult [20], a result that bears directly on a corpus that assigns one label per conversation. Koga et al. (2023) studied emotions that are elicited but not stated, showing that a substantial part of the problem is inference rather than classification [19]. From deployed systems, the Chirpy Cardinal open-domain agent reports a practical finding that we observe again in our own results: a RoBERTa classifier over the same 32 EmpatheticDialogues categories reaches about 61.5% accuracy, and its errors concentrate among neighbouring emotions rather than being spread at random [45].

The **clinical** end of the field uses the same machinery for screening rather than for replying. Lan et al. (2024) applied large language models to depression detection from social media text [22]; the CLPsych 2025 shared task framed the problem as capturing mental-health dynamics along a user's timeline [23]; and Kumar et al. (2024) surveyed the two obstacles that constrain the whole area, namely scarce labelled data and the bias that follows from it [25]. Against this, the systematic review by Sorin et al. (2024) is the most relevant caution for our own design: across the studies it examined, large language models were frequently rated as empathetic as or more empathetic than clinicians, but their empathy was repetitive, relying on a small set of stock formulations [24].

The closest work to this thesis, and the study we must position ourselves against, is the **Hybrid Empathetic Framework** of Yang et al. (2024), which augments a large language model with a small, specialised empathetic model and uses a two-stage emotion prediction strategy together with emotion-cause identification [46]. That is the same architectural intuition this thesis pursues: a small model that is good at emotion, feeding a large model that is good at language, with the emotional decision taken in a stage of its own. The differences are in what each system is built for. Their framework is aimed at benchmark performance on empathetic generation; ours is aimed at operating within 8 GB of memory on a consumer GPU, at exposing the complete emotional decision, including the candidate emotions and the words that produced them, as an audit trail attached to every reply, and at evaluating replies on what a support reply must do rather than on similarity to a reference. The existence of that paper means our contribution cannot be, and is not claimed to be, the idea of separating detection from generation; it is what that separation is made to deliver, and the evidence gathered for it.

Finally, the question of **how any of this should be judged** has become a literature of its own. Zheng et al. (2023) established that strong language models used as judges agree with human preferences over 80% of the time, while documenting their biases, including a preference for longer answers [28]. Panickssery et al. (2024) showed that such evaluators recognise and favour their own generations, which makes the choice of judge a methodological decision rather than a convenience [30]. Verga et al. (2024) proposed replacing a single large judge with a panel drawn from different model families, reporting lower intra-model bias at lower cost [29]. The WASSA shared task offers the complementary approach of a classifier trained directly on human empathy ratings [26], [27]. This thesis uses all three, and Section 5.1.5 describes the panel that results.

## 2.3 Summary of Key Findings

Six findings emerge from the work reviewed above, and together they define the space this thesis occupies.

**Pre-training matters more than architecture on this task:** The joint models improve from 30.6% to 41.2% emotion accuracy by adding mixtures of listeners, emotion mimicry, commonsense knowledge and graph reasoning [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], while RoBERTa-GPT2 reaches 51.5% simply by starting from pre-trained components [42]. The lesson we take from this is to spend the parameter budget on strong pre-trained models and on how they are connected, rather than on architectural machinery trained from scratch.

**Large language models write better replies than purpose-built empathetic models, but not more accurate ones:** Human raters prefer ChatGPT to CEM by a wide margin [13], yet evaluations of emotion recognition find large models no better than trained classifiers [21], weak at multi-label emotion [20], and challenged by emotions that are implied rather than stated [19]. This asymmetry, strong generation and unreliable recognition, is the direct argument for giving recognition to a separate model.

**Machine empathy repeats itself:** The characteristic weakness identified in systematic review is not incompetence but sameness: fluent, kind, and drawn from a small stock of phrases [24]. Few papers measure this, and none of the joint models above report it, which is why a repetition measure is included among our own signals.

**Structure helps, and there is more than one way to supply it:** Telling the model the emotion, asking it to reason before answering [13], [17], giving it a knowledge base to call [15], conditioning on personality [16], or retrieving related passages [40]: all of these add structure to an otherwise unconstrained generator, and all report gains. What has not been established is which of them is worth its cost under a hardware constraint, which is the comparison Chapter 5 carries out.

**Evaluation is the field's weakest point:** Published comparisons rest largely on overlap with a reference reply, which cannot tell whether a reply acknowledged the feeling or asked five questions. The alternatives now available, an empathy classifier trained on human ratings [26], [27] and panels of language-model judges drawn from different families [28], [29], [30], are what allow this thesis to be evaluated on what the replies do rather than on what they resemble.

**Separating the small model from the large one has been proposed, but for a different purpose:** The Hybrid Empathetic Framework pairs a small empathetic model with a large language model and predicts emotion in two stages [46]. Our work shares that structure and differs in its purpose: a complete system inside 8 GB, an emotional decision exposed as an auditable record rather than an internal step, and an evaluation built around rule adherence, emotional fidelity, a three-rater empathy panel and paired statistics.

**The gap this thesis addresses:** No existing work, to our knowledge, builds an empathetic response system that (i) runs entirely within consumer hardware so that a user's disclosure never leaves the machine, (ii) exposes every part of its emotional decision, from the candidate emotions and their confidence to the words that produced them, for inspection before a reply is generated, and (iii) is evaluated against single-model baselines of both comparable and larger size on measures that correspond to what a supportive reply must do, with every difference tested statistically. Chapters 4 and 5 build and evaluate exactly that system.
