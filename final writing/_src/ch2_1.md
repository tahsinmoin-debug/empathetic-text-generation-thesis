# 2.1 Preliminaries

**Empathy as something that can be measured:** In everyday use empathy is a vague word, and a system cannot be built against a vague word. The framework this work relies on is EPITOME, developed for text-based mental-health support and grounded in established empathy scales, which separates a reply into three communication mechanisms: *emotional reactions*, where the reply names and validates what the person feels; *interpretations*, where it shows it has understood the situation; and *explorations*, where it invites the person to say more {cite:sharma2020epitome}. Counselling practice adds a constraint from the other direction: guidance for motivational interviewing warns that stacking questions produces a "question-and-answer trap" in which the person becomes a passive respondent {cite:samhsa2019tip35}. Taken together these give a working definition of a good supportive reply that a machine can be held to: acknowledge the feeling, respond to the specific situation, and invite without interrogating.

**The corpus:** EmpatheticDialogues is the standard benchmark for this task {cite:rashkin2019}. One crowdworker was given one of 32 emotion labels, wrote a situation description, and then described that situation in conversation; a second worker replied as a listener without being told the label. The released files hold 24,850 conversations, divided by the authors into training, validation and test partitions with no speaker appearing in more than one {cite:ed_github}. Two properties of the corpus matter throughout this thesis: each conversation carries exactly one emotion label, chosen before the conversation was written; and the *situation description* and the *first message* are different texts, the former written about the event and the latter written to a listener.

**GoEmotions** is the other corpus commonly used for fine-grained emotion, containing about 58,000 Reddit comments labelled with 27 emotions plus neutral, and allowing more than one label per comment {cite:demszky2020}. This project began on GoEmotions and moved to EmpatheticDialogues; Section 4.3.1 gives the four measured reasons for that change.

**Recognising emotion in conversation:** Emotion recognition in conversation is a field in its own right, distinguished from sentence-level classification by the fact that the same words carry different feelings depending on what came before {cite:poria2019erc}. Related work maps the *intents* a listener's reply can express, such as questioning, acknowledging, consoling or encouraging, into a taxonomy derived from the same corpus {cite:welivita2020}, and shows that emotions in dialogue are frequently implicit, requiring inference rather than recognition {cite:koga2023implicit}, and frequently multiple, with a single utterance carrying more than one label {cite:belay2025}.

**The models:** Every model in this work descends from the transformer {cite:vaswani2017}. Two families are used for two different jobs. **Encoders** read a whole text at once and are trained with masked-language modelling, which makes them suited to classification: BERT established the pattern {cite:devlin2019} and RoBERTa improved it by training longer on far more text with dynamic masking {cite:liu2019roberta}. **Decoder-only language models** generate text left to right and, when instruction-tuned, follow written rules; this work uses Qwen2.5 {cite:qwen2024} and Phi-4-mini {cite:abouelenin2025phi4mini}, the latter trained with a particular emphasis on instruction following.

**Making large models fit:** Two techniques make a multi-billion-parameter generator usable on a consumer GPU. **Quantisation** stores weights at reduced precision; 4-bit NF4 with double quantisation retains the quality of 16-bit weights at roughly a quarter of the memory {cite:dettmers2023qlora}. **Low-rank adaptation** trains a small number of additional parameters instead of the whole network, making fine-tuning feasible on the same hardware {cite:hu2022lora}. A third technique, **retrieval-augmented generation**, supplies a model with relevant passages retrieved at inference time {cite:lewis2020rag}, typically found with sentence embeddings {cite:reimers2019sbert}; this work builds it as a baseline rather than as part of the final design.

**Measuring empathy:** Because no single automatic measure captures empathy, three kinds of evaluation are used here. The **WASSA shared task on empathy detection** provides a model trained on human empathy ratings of conversation turns {cite:giorgi2024wassa}, of which the hyy33 system is used in this work {cite:yang2024hyy33}. **Large language models as judges** correlate well with human preference when the judge is strong {cite:zheng2023judge}, but they favour their own generations {cite:panickssery2024}, which is why a panel drawn from different developers is recommended over a single judge {cite:verga2024}. **Human evaluation** remains the reference, and recent empathetic-generation work reports it as either five-point ratings {cite:qian2023llm} or head-to-head preference {cite:cao2025tooled}.
