# 5.1 Performance Evaluation

This section reports the performance of the final system on every evaluation signal, with all alternative designs shown for comparison. The reasons for the differences between designs are analysed in Section 5.2, and their statistical significance in Section 5.4.

## 5.1.1 Experimental Setup

All generation systems respond to the same 300 conversations, sampled with seed 42 from the EmpatheticDialogues test partition, using identical decoding settings (Section 4.4.2). The detector is evaluated on all 2,542 test conversations. The systems are referred to by the names given in Table 5.1, and the **final system** is the two-stage pipeline with a RoBERTa-base detector and a Phi-4-mini generator.

## 5.1.2 Emotion Detection

Table 5.2 reports the performance of the final detector. RoBERTa-base reaches **50.3% top-1**, **76.5% top-3** and **84.3% top-5** accuracy, with a macro-F1 score of 49.4%, against a chance level of 3.1% for 32 classes. Performance varies considerably across emotions (Table 5.3). *Caring* (F1 70.1%), *lonely* (69.5%) and *jealous* (64.7%) are recognised most reliably, while *angry* (18.2%), *ashamed* (22.2%) and *joyful* (30.9%) are the most difficult. Table 5.4 shows that the most frequent errors occur between closely related labels: *sentimental* is read as *nostalgic* (31 cases), *afraid* as *terrified* (29), *angry* as *furious* (24) and *sad* as *devastated* (23). Many errors are therefore differences in intensity or nuance within the same family of emotions rather than confusions between unrelated feelings, consistent with the error pattern reported for a comparable classifier {cite:paranjape2020chirpy}. This is why the final design passes the three most probable emotions to the generator rather than a single label.

## 5.1.3 Rule Adherence

Table 5.1 and Figure 5.1 show how often each system's replies acknowledge the user's feeling, ask exactly one question and stay within 120 words. The final system follows all three rules in **60.0%** of replies: 66.3% contain an acknowledgement, 92.7% ask exactly one question and 100% stay within the length limit, with an average length of 57.1 words. Among systems of comparable size, it is exceeded by the top-1 Phi-4-mini configuration (65.3%) and the initial pipeline (63.7%), and it is well ahead of the same-size single model (51.3%), the monolithic fine-tuned model (22.3%) and zero-shot prompting (19.0%). The larger single model, Qwen2.5-7B, has the highest rule adherence at 82.3%. The monolithic fine-tuned model reproduces the style of the listener replies it was trained on: its replies average 10.0 words, 74.0% ask exactly one question, but only 30.0% acknowledge the user's feeling. Real EmpatheticDialogues listener replies behave the same way, satisfying all three rules in only 4.3% of cases and acknowledging the feeling in 10.6%.

## 5.1.4 Empathy Classifier Score

Figure 5.2 shows the WASSA empathy scores with 95% confidence intervals. The final system scores **5.05**, the highest of all systems no larger than Phi-4-mini, and above the same-size single model (4.84), the initial pipeline (4.80), zero-shot prompting (4.25) and the fine-tuned model (3.36). The fine-tuned model's low score partly reflects the length of its replies, since WASSA scores rise with reply length (Section 5.5.4). Gemma-3-4B (5.30) and the larger single model (5.76) score higher, and Section 5.5 shows that Gemma's advantage is explained by the length of its replies. The classifier outputs coarse ordinal classes, and our replies fall between classes 1 and 7. It was also trained on empathy ratings of turns in peer discussions of news articles. We therefore interpret differences between system averages rather than absolute values; Section 5.4.6 shows that it ranks the systems in nearly the same order as the AI judges.

## 5.1.5 Emotion Fidelity

To check whether a reply expresses the *right* emotion, and not merely a well-formed one, each reply is passed back through the final detector. We compare the detector's reading of the reply with the true label (**emotion validation**) and with the emotion the system conditioned on (**context awareness**). The final system has the highest emotion validation of all systems at **36.7%**, compared with 32.3% for the larger single model, 28.3% for the top-1 Phi-4-mini configuration, 26.0% for the initial pipeline and 25.7% for the same-size single model. Its context awareness is 54.3%. Two limitations apply to these measures. The detector was trained on user messages rather than replies, so its reading of a reply is noisy. And context awareness uses the same detector that supplied the signal to our pipelines, which may favour them. Emotion validation, which compares against the dataset's independent gold label, is therefore the more trustworthy of the two.

## 5.1.6 Behaviour of the Two-Stage Emotion Selection

Table 5.5 examines the stage-1 decision. The true emotion is among the detector's three best candidates in 78.0% of the 300 evaluation conversations, and every stage-1 output names a valid candidate. The generator overrides the detector's top choice in 40.7% of cases, and its choice matches the gold label in **42.3%** of conversations. This is *lower* than the detector's own top-1 accuracy on the same conversations (50.3%). The final system's gains in empathy and emotion validation therefore do not come from selecting the gold label more often, a finding discussed in Section 5.6.

## 5.1.7 Diversity and Repetition

Table 5.6 reports distinct-n diversity together with a boilerplate rate, defined as the proportion of replies that contain one of the system's ten most frequent five-word phrases. The final system's Dist-2 of 47.8% is close to that of the initial pipeline (49.5%), but its boilerplate rate is higher (58.7% against 23.7%). Its most repeated phrase, "how are you feeling right", appears in 49 replies. Phi-4-mini prompted with a single generic prompt is the most repetitive system (77.0%), so emotion-specific prompts reduce repetition for the same generator but do not eliminate it, consistent with the repetitive empathic phrasing reported for LLMs {cite:sorin2024jmir}. Because these values are computed over 300 replies, they are not comparable with distinct-n scores published for the full test set.

## 5.1.8 AI-Judge Evaluation

Two AI judges, Nemotron 3 Ultra and gpt-oss-120b, selected as described in Section 4.1.3.1, score each reply blind from 1 to 10 for empathy, relevance and safety, and the panel score is the mean of the two judges. They are applied to a stratified sample of 64 conversations, consisting of the shortest and longest message for each of the 32 emotions. Table 5.7 reports the results for all eight judged systems. The larger single model has the highest overall score (**8.90**), followed by the single model built on our own generator (8.78), the **final system (8.68)** and top-1 Phi-4-mini prompting (8.65); then come the same-size single model (8.24), the initial pipeline (8.04), zero-shot prompting (7.51) and the fine-tuned model (5.96). The four leading systems lie within 0.22 points of one another, and Section 5.4.7 shows that the differences among them are not significant. The final system scores 7.84 for empathy, 8.45 for relevance and 9.76 for safety. Empathy is the lowest of the three dimensions for every system, which indicates that the judges treat it as the most demanding criterion. Safety scores are high for every judged system (9.31 to 9.86) except the fine-tuned model (8.67). The two judges differ in severity: Nemotron 3 Ultra gives a lower average score than gpt-oss-120b to every system. Section 5.4.7 tests which of these differences are statistically significant and how closely the judges agree.

**What the judge ranking settles and what it does not:** the final system is third of eight on the panel, and that position has to be read together with what the panel is able to separate. The four leading systems all use a generator of 3.8 billion parameters or more, they lie within 0.22 points of one another, and none of the differences among them survives correction (Section 5.4.7); the judges also award higher scores to longer replies (Section 5.5.4), and of the four the final system writes the shortest ones. What the panel does separate is scale: every system below the four leaders uses a generator of 1.5 billion parameters or fewer. The decision to keep the two-stage design rather than prompt the same generator directly was therefore not taken on the judge scores, which rate the two configurations equally (8.68 against 8.78, p = 0.16), but on the measures that do distinguish them: emotion validation (36.7% against 27.0%), the WASSA empathy classifier (5.05 against 4.75, an advantage that strengthens when reply length is held constant), repetition (boilerplate in 58.7% of replies against 72.3%), length (22% fewer words) and the fact that the emotional decision is visible and can be logged or overridden, set against a cost of 13.7 points of rule adherence. Section 5.5.9 and Table 5.29 give that comparison measure by measure, and Section 5.2.6 combines five of the measures into one ranking in which the final system stands second of eight and first among the systems that fit the 8 GB budget.

## 5.1.9 Human Evaluation

To validate the automatic and AI-judge results, we conducted a rating study with human participants (Table 5.8). Participants read 8 situations; for each one they saw three replies, presented in counterbalanced order, from the final system, the same-size single model and the larger single model. They rated every reply from 1 to 5 on three dimensions, each with written anchors: **empathy and emotional validation** ("how well does this reply recognise and validate the emotions expressed in the message?"), **contextual relevance** ("how well does this reply address the person's specific situation rather than giving a generic response?") and **supportiveness** ("if you had written this message, how supported or comforted would you feel by this reply?"). Each situation ends with one preference question, which keeps the head-to-head comparison used in similar studies {cite:cao2025tooled, wu2025traits} alongside the ratings. Rating rather than choosing gives a score for every system on every situation, so the size of a difference can be measured and not only its direction. The situations cover the four emotions most central to supportive conversation (sad, lonely, angry and anxious), three drawn from student life and one positive emotion; five of the eight are also scored by the AI judges, which is what allows human and AI ratings to be compared. The study contains no attention-check items: with 24 ratings per participant, the design relies on a short questionnaire and a small group of committed volunteers rather than on decoy replies.
