# 5.1 Performance Evaluation

Every design built in Chapter 4 answered the same conversations and was measured with the same five signals. This section reports what each achieved; why they differ is analysed in Section 5.2, and which differences are large enough to trust in Section 5.4. Tables carrying a letter, such as Table A.7, are the supporting tables collected in the appendix; every claim in this chapter can be read without them.

## 5.1.1 Experimental Setup and the Systems Compared

All twelve generation systems answer the same 300 conversations, drawn with seed 42 from the EmpatheticDialogues test partition, with identical decoding settings (Section 4.4.2). The detector is measured on all 2,542 test conversations. Nothing else changes between runs, so any difference is a property of the design rather than of the sample.

Table 5.1 is the scoreboard for the chapter, and its seven columns are used throughout. **Ack** is the share of replies that acknowledge the feeling, from a keyword check; **one question** the share asking exactly one; **all rules** the share passing all three checks at once, which is the strictest column and the one quoted in the text. **Mean words** is the average reply length, which matters because both the empathy classifier and the judges reward length (Section 5.4.4). **WASSA empathy** is the 0-10 score of an independent empathy classifier, and **emotion validation** the share of replies that, read back by the detector, express the conversation's true emotion. Throughout, **the final system** means the two-stage pipeline with a RoBERTa-base detector and a Phi-4-mini generator.

## 5.1.2 Emotion Detection

The detector reaches **50.3% top-1**, **76.5% top-3** and **84.3% top-5** accuracy over 32 emotions, with a macro-F1 of 49.4% against a chance level of 3.1%. Accuracy is uneven across the set: *caring* (F1 70.1%), *lonely* (69.5%) and *jealous* (64.7%) are recognised reliably, while *angry* (18.2%), *ashamed* (22.2%) and *joyful* (30.9%) are hardest, a spread of 52 points with 7 of the 32 emotions below 40%. Macro-F1 is reported for exactly that reason: it counts every emotion equally, so a detector scoring well by neglecting the harder classes could not hide behind its accuracy. Accuracy, weighted-F1 (49.7%) and macro-F1 agree to within 0.9 points, which says the result is not produced by the commoner emotions; the test partition is close to balanced, at 50 to 127 conversations per emotion. Per-emotion figures for all 32 classes are in Table 5.2.

The shape of the errors matters more than their number. The twelve most frequent mistakes (Table 5.3) are every one of them a near neighbour: *sentimental* read as *nostalgic*, *afraid* as *terrified*, *angry* as *furious*, *sad* as *devastated*. These are differences of intensity within one family of feeling, not confusions between unrelated emotions, the same pattern reported for a comparable classifier {cite:paranjape2020chirpy}. Two consequences shaped the design: a reply built on the wrong label is rarely built on the wrong *kind* of feeling, and the correct label is far more often inside the top three than at the top, which is why three candidates are passed to the generator instead of one.

## 5.1.3 Rules, Empathy and Emotional Fidelity

Figure 5.2 carries the three headline measures for every system.

**Rule adherence:** a reply passes only if it acknowledges the feeling, asks exactly one question and stays within 120 words. The final system passes in **60.0%** of replies, from 66.3% acknowledgement, 92.7% exactly one question and 100% within the limit, averaging 57.1 words. Among systems of its own scale it sits behind top-1 Phi-4-mini prompting (65.3%) and the initial pipeline (63.7%), well ahead of the same-size single model (51.3%), the fine-tuned model (22.3%) and zero-shot prompting (19.0%); the largest single model leads at 82.3%. Two results are worth reading carefully. The fine-tuned model reproduces the style it was trained on rather than the behaviour the rules ask for, averaging 10.0 words and acknowledging the feeling in only 30.0% of replies, which is faithful learning, because the human listener replies it was trained on satisfy all three rules in 4.3% of cases. And Gemma-3-4B fails almost entirely (22.0%) not because its replies are poor but because it ignores the length limit at 133 words (Section 5.2.3).

**Empathy:** the empathy measure is not ours, and that is the point of using it. It is the hyy33 system from the WASSA 2024 shared task {cite:yang2024hyy33, giorgi2024wassa}, a transformer trained on conversation turns that people had rated for empathy, returning one score from 0 to 10. It shares no model family with any generator compared here, so it has no way to prefer our design, and Section 5.4.4 tests rather than assumes it. The final system scores **5.05**, the highest of any system no larger than Phi-4-mini, above the same-size single model (4.84), the initial pipeline (4.80) and zero-shot prompting (4.25); only Gemma-3-4B (5.30) and the 7.6-billion-parameter single model (5.76) score higher.

**Emotional fidelity:** a reply can be well formed and still answer the wrong feeling, so each reply is passed back through the detector and compared with the conversation's true label. The final system is the highest of all twelve at **36.7%**, ahead of the largest single model (32.3%), top-1 Phi-4-mini prompting (28.3%) and the same-size single model (25.7%). A second measure, context awareness, compares the reply with the emotion the system conditioned on, where it reaches 54.3%; we prefer the first, because the detector reads replies it was never trained on and because context awareness uses the very detector that fed our pipelines.

## 5.1.4 Inside the Two-Stage Decision, and Repetition

The true emotion is among the detector's three candidates in 78.0% of the evaluation conversations, every stage-1 answer named one of the three, so the fallback was never needed, and the generator overrides the detector's first choice in 40.7% of cases. Its final choice matches the gold label in **42.3%** of conversations, which is *lower* than the detector's own 50.3% on the same conversations, and reliably so (Section 5.4.3). The two-stage design therefore does not earn its gains by picking the gold label more often; Section 5.6.2 explains what it does instead.

Repetitiveness is the most common complaint about machine-written empathy {cite:sorin2024jmir}, so we measure it as a **boilerplate rate**: the share of replies containing one of that system's ten most frequent five-word phrases. The final system's rate is 58.7%, with "how are you feeling right" appearing in 49 replies. The comparison that matters is the same generator prompted generically, at 77.0%: per-emotion prompts reduce repetition in an identical model without curing it.

## 5.1.5 The AI-Judge Panel

**Why these two models:** three requirements fixed the panel. A judge had to be flagship-scale, because only strong judges track human preference reliably {cite:zheng2023judge}; it could not share a family with anything it judges, because LLM evaluators favour their own generations {cite:panickssery2024}; and one judge is not enough, because a panel from different developers shows less of any single model's bias {cite:verga2024}. Nemotron 3 Ultra (NVIDIA) and gpt-oss-120b (OpenAI, open weights) meet all three, neither is related to Phi-4-mini (Microsoft) or Qwen (Alibaba), and both are reachable on free tiers, which a project with no compute budget needs. Four further candidates were prepared and lost to circumstances outside the work (Table 5.4).

Each judge scores every reply from 1 to 10 for empathy, relevance and safety, and the panel score is the mean of the two. The ordering is led by the largest single model (**8.90**), then the single model built on our own generator (8.78), the **final system (8.68)** and top-1 Phi-4-mini prompting (8.65), followed by the same-size single model (8.24), the initial pipeline (8.04), zero-shot prompting (7.51) and the fine-tuned model (5.96). One pattern repeats in every system: safety is easy to satisfy (9.31 to 9.86 for all but the fine-tuned model), relevance is harder, and empathy hardest; the final system scores 7.84, 8.45 and 9.76.

**How we checked that the scoring was fair:** Figure 5.3 prints the prompt a judge receives and the five controls around it. The judge sees the user's message and one reply and nothing else, so it cannot know which design wrote the text or whether that design is ours. Both judges scored all 512 replies, so no system was rated by a different judge from another. The rubric fixes what each band of the scale means, the temperature is zero so the same reply always receives the same score, and the answer is constrained to JSON with every parse recorded. Finally the judges are compared against each other rather than trusted: they agree at rho = 0.69 on empathy and order the eight systems the same way (Section 5.4.5).

**Every emotion was judged, not a favourable subset:** the sample holds two conversations for each of the 32 emotions, the shortest and the longest message in that category, so no emotion is missing and no length band is over-represented; the judged messages run from 4 to 54 words. Figure 5.4 gives the final system's judge empathy for each emotion, and the spread is wide and patterned: above its own mean on acute distress (*terrified* 9.0, *sad*, *devastated*, *afraid*, *angry*), below it on positive or settled states (*confident* 5.5, *trusting*, *grateful*, *hopeful*). That is what a system built for support should look like, and it also marks where it is weakest. With two conversations behind each value, these are indicative and no significance is claimed for them.

**What the ranking settles, and what it does not:** the final system is third of eight, and that has to be read against what the panel can separate at all. The four leaders all use a generator of 3.8 billion parameters or more, lie within 0.22 points of one another, and none of the differences among them survives correction (Section 5.4.5); what the panel does separate is scale, since every system below them uses 1.5 billion parameters or fewer. The judges therefore rate the two-stage design and the same generator prompted directly as equal (8.68 against 8.78, p = 0.16), and the choice between them rests on the measures that do separate them (Section 5.5.5).

## 5.1.6 Human Evaluation

To check the automatic measures against people, we ran a rating study. Participants read 8 situations; for each they saw three replies in counterbalanced order, from the final system, the same-size single model and the largest single model, and rated each from 1 to 5 on three dimensions with written anchors: **empathy and emotional validation**, how well the reply recognises and validates the feeling; **contextual relevance**, how well it addresses this person's situation rather than giving a generic answer; and **supportiveness**, how supported the reader would feel on receiving it. Each situation ends with a preference question, which keeps the head-to-head comparison used in similar studies {cite:cao2025tooled} beside the ratings; rating as well as choosing gives every system a score on every situation, so the size of a difference can be measured and not only its direction. The situations cover the four emotions most central to supportive conversation and five of the eight are also scored by the AI judges, which is what lets the two rankings be compared. ## 5.1.7 The System at Work

Numbers say how often something happens; they do not show what the system does. Two figures close this section with the system running on real input, printing its audit trail.

**The detector (Figure 5.5):** for each message it returns three candidates with their confidence and the words its attention selected: *lonely* at 91.9% for someone who moved city and stopped speaking to people, and *devastated* at 54.3% ahead of *sad* at 30.9% for a dog lost while its owner was away, where the attention picks out "My dog disappeared" and "and nobody told". Those candidates and cue words are exactly what the generator receives next.

**The agent (Figure 5.6):** three turns of a conversation about having no time for one's parents. The trail above each reply shows the detector's window widening as the conversation grows, from one message to three, and the emotion it read at each turn: *guilty* at 56%, then *guilty* again at 67% once the second message is read together with the first, then *apprehensive* at 35% when the person asks whether they should talk to their parents, where the agent announces the change, guilty to apprehensive, and answers the feeling the person has now. Every reply obeys the rules it was given: 52, 44 and 49 words, exactly one question each. Neither figure is a mock-up; both print the record the system keeps for every turn.
