# 5.1 Performance Evaluation

Every design built in Chapter 4 was run on the same conversations and measured with the same five signals. This section reports what each one achieved. Why the designs differ is analysed in Section 5.2, and which differences are large enough to trust is settled in Section 5.4.

## 5.1.1 Experimental Setup and the Systems Compared

All twelve generation systems answer the same 300 conversations, drawn with seed 42 from the EmpatheticDialogues test partition, with identical decoding settings (Section 4.4.2). The detector is measured on all 2,542 test conversations. Nothing else changes between runs, so any difference is a property of the design and not of the sample or the sampling temperature. Table 5.1 is the scoreboard for the chapter: it holds every system, the three rule checks, reply length, the empathy score and emotion fidelity in one place, and later sections take their numbers from it. Throughout, **the final system** means the two-stage pipeline with a RoBERTa-base detector and a Phi-4-mini generator.

Its seven columns are used throughout the chapter and each means one thing. **Ack** is the share of replies that acknowledge the feeling, from a keyword check; **one question** the share asking exactly one; **all rules** the share passing all three checks at once, which is the strictest column and the one quoted in the text. **Mean words** is the average length of a reply, which matters because both the empathy classifier and the judges reward length (Section 5.4.4). **WASSA empathy** is the 0-10 score of an independent empathy classifier (Section 5.1.4), and **emotion validation** the share of replies that, read back by the detector, express the conversation's true emotion (Section 5.1.5).

## 5.1.2 Emotion Detection

The detector reaches **50.3% top-1**, **76.5% top-3** and **84.3% top-5** accuracy over 32 emotions, with a macro-F1 of 49.4% against a chance level of 3.1% (Table 5.2). Accuracy is uneven across the emotion set: *caring* (F1 70.1%), *lonely* (69.5%) and *jealous* (64.7%) are recognised reliably, while *angry* (18.2%), *ashamed* (22.2%) and *joyful* (30.9%) are the hardest, a spread of 52 points with 7 of the 32 emotions below 40%. Macro-F1 is reported for exactly that reason: it counts every emotion equally, so a detector that scored well by neglecting the harder classes could not hide behind its accuracy. Accuracy (50.3%), weighted-F1 (49.7%) and macro-F1 (49.4%) agree to within 0.9 points, which says the result is not produced by the commoner emotions; the test partition is close to balanced, with 50 to 127 conversations per emotion. Per-emotion figures for all 32 classes are in Table 5.3.

The shape of the errors matters more than their number. Figure 5.1 shows the twelve most frequent mistakes, and every one of them is a near neighbour: *sentimental* read as *nostalgic*, *afraid* as *terrified*, *angry* as *furious*, *sad* as *devastated*. These are differences of intensity or shade within one family of feeling, not confusions between unrelated emotions, which is the same error pattern reported for a comparable classifier {cite:paranjape2020chirpy}. Two consequences follow, and both shaped the design: the correct emotion is usually close to the predicted one, so a reply built on the wrong label is rarely built on the wrong *kind* of feeling; and the correct label is far more often inside the top three than at the top, which is why the final design passes three candidates to the generator instead of one.

## 5.1.3 Following the Three Response Rules

A reply passes the rule check only if it acknowledges the feeling, asks exactly one question and stays within 120 words. The left panel of Figure 5.2 shows how often each system manages all three. The final system passes in **60.0%** of replies, built from 66.3% acknowledgement, 92.7% exactly one question and 100% within the limit, at an average of 57.1 words. Among systems of its own scale it sits behind top-1 Phi-4-mini prompting (65.3%) and the initial pipeline (63.7%), and well ahead of the same-size single model (51.3%), the fine-tuned model (22.3%) and zero-shot prompting (19.0%). The largest single model, Qwen2.5-7B, leads every system at 82.3%.

Two results are worth reading carefully. The fine-tuned model reproduces the style of the replies it was trained on rather than the behaviour the rules ask for: 10.0 words on average, usually one question (74.0%), but acknowledgement in only 30.0%. That is faithful learning, because the human listener replies it was trained on behave the same way, satisfying all three rules in 4.3% of cases. And Gemma-3-4B fails almost entirely (22.0%) not because its replies are poor but because it ignores the length limit, averaging 133 words (Section 5.2.3).

## 5.1.4 Empathy Classifier Score

The empathy measure is not ours, and that is the point of using it. It is the hyy33 system from the WASSA 2024 shared task on empathy detection {cite:yang2024hyy33, giorgi2024wassa}, a transformer trained on conversation turns that people had rated for empathy, which returns one score from 0 to 10 for a piece of text. It was built and peer-reviewed for this exact purpose, it knows nothing about our systems, and it shares no model family with any generator compared here, so it has no way to prefer our design. Section 5.4.5 tests it rather than assuming it: it correlates with human empathy ratings at r = 0.544, and across our systems it ranks them much as the AI judges do (rho = 0.74). The middle panel of Figure 5.2 gives every system's mean with a 95% confidence interval, so that each difference can be read against the uncertainty of the estimate behind it. The final system scores **5.05**, the highest of any system no larger than Phi-4-mini, above the same-size single model (4.84), the initial pipeline (4.80), zero-shot prompting (4.25) and the fine-tuned model (3.36). Two systems score higher: Gemma-3-4B (5.30) and the 7.6-billion-parameter single model (5.76).

The classifier has to be read with care: it was trained on empathy ratings of peer discussions of news articles, it produces coarse classes, and it rewards longer replies (Section 5.4.4). We therefore use it only to compare system averages over 300 replies, never to judge a single reply, and always beside the judges and emotion fidelity. Section 5.4.5 sets out the evidence for and against trusting it.

## 5.1.5 Emotion Fidelity

A reply can be well formed and still answer the wrong feeling. To test this, each reply is passed back through the detector and its reading is compared with the conversation's true label, a measure we call **emotion validation** (right panel of Figure 5.2). The final system is the highest of all twelve systems at **36.7%**, ahead of the largest single model (32.3%), top-1 Phi-4-mini prompting (28.3%), the initial pipeline (26.0%) and the same-size single model (25.7%). A second measure, **context awareness**, compares the reply with the emotion the system itself conditioned on; the final system reaches 54.3% there.

Both measures have limits and we prefer the first. The detector was trained on user messages, not on replies, so its reading of a reply is noisy; and context awareness uses the very detector that fed our own pipelines, which favours them. Emotion validation is checked against the dataset's independent label, so it is the more trustworthy of the two.

## 5.1.6 Inside the Two-Stage Decision

Table 5.5 opens up the stage-1 choice. The true emotion is among the detector's three candidates in 78.0% of the 300 evaluation conversations, and every stage-1 answer named one of the three, so the fallback was never needed. The generator overrides the detector's first choice in 40.7% of conversations, and its final choice matches the gold label in **42.3%** of them.

That is *lower* than the detector's own top-1 accuracy on the same conversations (50.3%), and the difference is statistically reliable (Section 5.4.3). The two-stage design therefore does not earn its gains in empathy and emotion fidelity by picking the gold label more often. Section 5.6.2 explains what it does instead.

## 5.1.7 Diversity and Repetition

Repetitiveness is the most common complaint about machine-written empathy {cite:sorin2024jmir}, so we measure it directly (Table 5.6). Beside distinct-n diversity we report a **boilerplate rate**: the share of replies containing one of that system's ten most frequent five-word phrases. The final system's rate is 58.7%, with "how are you feeling right" appearing in 49 replies. The comparison that matters is with the same generator prompted generically, which reaches 77.0%: per-emotion prompts reduce repetition in an identical model without curing it. Computed over 300 replies, these values cannot be compared with distinct-n scores published for a full test set.

## 5.1.8 The AI-Judge Panel

**Why these two models:** three requirements fixed the panel. A judge had to be flagship-scale, because only strong judges track human preference reliably {cite:zheng2023judge}; it could not share a family with anything it judges, because LLM evaluators recognise and favour their own generations {cite:panickssery2024}; and one judge is not enough, because a panel drawn from different developers shows less of any single model's bias {cite:verga2024}. Nemotron 3 Ultra (NVIDIA) and gpt-oss-120b (OpenAI, open weights) meet all three, and neither is related to Phi-4-mini (Microsoft) or Qwen (Alibaba). Both are reachable on free tiers, which a project with no compute budget needs. Four other candidates were prepared and lost to circumstances outside the work, listed with the rest of the selection criteria in Table 5.38.

Each judge scores every reply from 1 to 10 for empathy, relevance and safety, and the panel score is the mean of the two. They read a stratified sample of 64 conversations, the shortest and the longest message for each of the 32 emotions. Table 5.7 gives the results for all eight judged systems and Figure 5.3 breaks them down by dimension.

The ordering is led by the largest single model (**8.90**), then the single model built on our own generator (8.78), the **final system (8.68)** and top-1 Phi-4-mini prompting (8.65), followed by the same-size single model (8.24), the initial pipeline (8.04), zero-shot prompting (7.51) and the fine-tuned model (5.96). One pattern repeats in every system: safety is easy to satisfy (9.31 to 9.86 for all but the fine-tuned model), relevance is harder, and empathy is hardest. The final system scores 7.84 for empathy, 8.45 for relevance and 9.76 for safety.

**What the ranking settles, and what it does not:** the final system is third of eight, and that has to be read against what the panel can separate at all. The four leaders all use a generator of 3.8 billion parameters or more, lie within 0.22 points of one another, and none of the differences among them survives correction (Section 5.4.6); the judges also reward length (Section 5.4.4), and of the four the final system writes the shortest replies. What the panel does separate is scale, since every system below those four uses 1.5 billion parameters or fewer. The judges therefore rate the two-stage design and the same generator prompted directly as equal (8.68 against 8.78, p = 0.16), and the choice between them rests on the measures that do separate them (Section 5.5.7).

**How we checked that the scoring was fair:** Figure 5.12 prints the prompt a judge receives and the five controls around it. The judge sees the user's message and one reply and nothing else, so it cannot know which design wrote the text, whether that design is ours, or what a human replied. Both judges scored all 512 replies, so no system was rated by a different judge from another. The rubric fixes what each band of the scale means, the temperature is zero so the same reply always receives the same score, and the answer is constrained to JSON with every parse recorded. Finally the two judges are compared against each other rather than trusted: they agree at rho = 0.69 on empathy and order the eight systems the same way (Section 5.4.6).

## 5.1.9 Human Evaluation

To check the automatic measures against people, we ran a rating study. Participants read 8 situations; for each one they saw three replies in counterbalanced order, from the final system, the same-size single model and the largest single model, and rated each reply from 1 to 5 on three dimensions with written anchors: **empathy and emotional validation**, how well the reply recognises and validates the feeling; **contextual relevance**, how well it addresses this person's situation rather than giving a generic answer; and **supportiveness**, how supported the reader would feel on receiving it. Each situation ends with a preference question, which keeps the head-to-head comparison used in similar studies {cite:cao2025tooled} beside the ratings.

Rating rather than only choosing gives every system a score on every situation, so the size of a difference can be measured and not only its direction. The situations cover the four emotions most central to supportive conversation (sad, lonely, angry and anxious), three drawn from student life and one positive emotion; five of the eight are also scored by the AI judges, which is what lets the human and AI rankings be compared. There are no attention-check items: with 24 ratings each, the study relies on a short questionnaire and committed volunteers rather than on decoy replies. Table 5.8 gives the item-by-item design.
