# 2.3 Summary of Key Findings

Six findings emerge from the work reviewed above, and together they define the space this thesis occupies.

**Pre-training matters more than architecture on this task:** The joint models improve from 30.6% to 41.2% emotion accuracy by adding mixtures of listeners, emotion mimicry, commonsense knowledge and graph reasoning {cite:lin2019moel, majumder2020mime, li2020empdg, li2022kemp, sabour2022cem, zhou2023case, yang2023escm}, while RoBERTa-GPT2 reaches 51.5% simply by starting from pre-trained components {cite:liu2021robertagpt2}. The lesson we take from this is to spend the parameter budget on strong pre-trained models and on how they are connected, rather than on architectural machinery trained from scratch.

**Large language models write better replies than purpose-built empathetic models, but not more accurate ones:** Human raters prefer ChatGPT to CEM by a wide margin {cite:qian2023llm}, yet evaluations of emotion recognition find large models no better than trained classifiers {cite:feng2023affect}, weak at multi-label emotion {cite:belay2025}, and challenged by emotions that are implied rather than stated {cite:koga2023implicit}. This asymmetry, strong generation and unreliable recognition, is the direct argument for giving recognition to a separate model.

**Machine empathy repeats itself:** The characteristic weakness identified in systematic review is not incompetence but sameness: fluent, kind, and drawn from a small stock of phrases {cite:sorin2024jmir}. Few papers measure this, and none of the joint models above report it, which is why a repetition measure is included among our own signals.

**Structure helps, and there is more than one way to supply it:** Telling the model the emotion, asking it to reason before answering {cite:qian2023llm, li2024emotioncot}, giving it a knowledge base to call {cite:cao2025tooled}, conditioning on personality {cite:wu2025traits}, or retrieving related passages {cite:lewis2020rag}: all of these add structure to an otherwise unconstrained generator, and all report gains. What has not been established is which of them is worth its cost under a hardware constraint, which is the comparison Chapter 5 carries out.

**Evaluation is the field's weakest point:** Published comparisons rest largely on overlap with a reference reply, which cannot tell whether a reply acknowledged the feeling or asked five questions. The alternatives now available, an empathy classifier trained on human ratings {cite:giorgi2024wassa, yang2024hyy33} and panels of language-model judges drawn from different families {cite:zheng2023judge, verga2024, panickssery2024}, are what allow this thesis to be evaluated on what the replies do rather than on what they resemble.

**Separating the small model from the large one has been proposed, but for a different purpose:** The Hybrid Empathetic Framework pairs a small empathetic model with a large language model and predicts emotion in two stages {cite:yang2024hef}. Our work shares that structure and differs in its purpose: a complete system inside 8 GB, an emotional decision exposed as an auditable record rather than an internal step, and an evaluation built around rule adherence, emotional fidelity, a three-rater empathy panel and paired statistics.

**The gap this thesis addresses:** No existing work, to our knowledge, builds an empathetic response system that (i) runs entirely within consumer hardware so that a user's disclosure never leaves the machine, (ii) exposes every part of its emotional decision, from the candidate emotions and their confidence to the words that produced them, for inspection before a reply is generated, and (iii) is evaluated against single-model baselines of both comparable and larger size on measures that correspond to what a supportive reply must do, with every difference tested statistically. Chapters 4 and 5 build and evaluate exactly that system.
